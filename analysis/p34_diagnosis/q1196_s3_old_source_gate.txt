[_Comte_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Count "Count")**Henri Marie Raymond de Toulouse-Lautrec-Montfa**[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-Acte_naissance-1) (24 November 1864– 9 September 1901), known as **Toulouse-Lautrec** (French:[[tuluz lotʁɛk]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/French "Help:IPA/French")), was a French painter, printmaker, draughtsman, caricaturist, and illustrator. His immersion in the colourful and theatrical life of [Paris's Belle Époque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_in_the_Belle_%C3%89poque "Paris in the Belle Époque") in the late 19th century allowed him to produce popular works of art from decadent affairs.

Born into the aristocracy, Toulouse-Lautrec broke both his legs during adolescence, leaving him with a stunted appearance. In later life, he developed an affinity for [brothels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brothel "Brothel") and prostitutes that directed the subject matter for many of his works, which record details of the late-19th-century bohemian lifestyle in Paris. He is among the painters described as [Post-Impressionists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-Impressionism "Post-Impressionism"), with [Paul Cézanne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_C%C3%A9zanne "Paul Cézanne"), [Vincent van Gogh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincent_van_Gogh "Vincent van Gogh"), [Paul Gauguin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Gauguin "Paul Gauguin"), and [Georges Seurat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Seurat "Georges Seurat") also commonly considered as belonging in this loose group.

Henri Marie Raymond de Toulouse-Lautrec-Montfa[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-2) was born at Demoiselles d'Imbert du Bosc (his great-aunts) home at rue de l'École Mage (presently 14, rue Henri de Toulouse Lautrec), Albi,[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-Acte_naissance-1)[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-3) in the south of France, the firstborn child of [Count Alphonse de Toulouse-Lautrec Montfa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Count_Alphonse_de_Toulouse-Lautrec_Montfa "Count Alphonse de Toulouse-Lautrec Montfa") (1838–1913)[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-4) and Adèle Zoë Tapié de Celeyran (1841–1930).[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-5) He was a member of an aristocratic family (descended from both the [Counts of Toulouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Count_of_Toulouse "Count of Toulouse") and [Odet de Foix, Vicomte de Lautrec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odet_of_Foix,_Viscount_of_Lautrec "Odet of Foix, Viscount of Lautrec"), as well as the [Viscounts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viscount "Viscount") of [Montfa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montfa,_Tarn "Montfa, Tarn")). His younger brother was born in 1867 but died the following year.

Both sons enjoyed the [_titres de courtoisie_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Courtesy_title "Courtesy title") of _[Comte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Count "Count")_.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-6) If Toulouse-Lautrec had outlived his father, he would have inherited the family [title](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobility_of_France "Nobility of France") of _Comte de Toulouse-Lautrec_.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-7)
After the death of his brother, Toulouse-Lautrec's parents separated, and a nanny cared for him.[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-TL-Book1-8) At the age of eight, Toulouse-Lautrec lived with his mother in Paris, where he drew sketches and caricatures in his exercise workbooks. A friend of his father, [René Princeteau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ren%C3%A9_Princeteau "René Princeteau"), sometimes visited to give informal lessons. Some of Toulouse-Lautrec's early paintings are of horses, a speciality of Princeteau's and a subject Toulouse-Lautrec later revisited in his "Circus Paintings".[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-TL-Book1-8)[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-9)
In 1875, Toulouse-Lautrec returned to Albi because his mother had concerns about his health. He took thermal baths at [Amélie-les-Bains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Am%C3%A9lie-les-Bains "Amélie-les-Bains"), and his mother consulted doctors in the hope of finding a way to improve her son's growth and development.[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-TL-Book1-8)

Toulouse-Lautrec's parents were [first cousins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_cousins "First cousins") (their mothers were sisters),[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-10) and his [congenital health conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congenital_disorder "Congenital disorder") have often been attributed to a family history of [inbreeding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inbreeding "Inbreeding").[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-11)

### Disability and health problems
At the age of 13, Toulouse-Lautrec fractured his right [femur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Femur "Femur"), and at age 14, he fractured his left femur.[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-TheTimes-12) The breaks did not heal properly. Modern physicians attribute this to an unknown [genetic disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_disorder "Genetic disorder"), possibly [pycnodysostosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pycnodysostosis "Pycnodysostosis") (sometimes known as 'Toulouse-Lautrec Syndrome'),[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-Valdes-Socin_2021-13)[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-Angier-14) or a variant disorder along the lines of [osteopetrosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osteopetrosis "Osteopetrosis"), [achondroplasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achondroplasia "Achondroplasia"), or [osteogenesis imperfecta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osteogenesis_imperfecta "Osteogenesis imperfecta").[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-15) Toulouse-Lautrec's legs ceased to grow when he reached 1.52 m (5 ft 0 in).[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-16) He developed an adult torso while retaining his child-sized legs.[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-17)

### Disability and health problems
During a stay in [Nice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nice,_France "Nice, France") on the [French Riviera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Riviera "French Riviera"), his progress in painting and drawing impressed Princeteau, who persuaded Toulouse-Lautrec's parents to allow him to return to Paris and study under the portrait painter [Léon Bonnat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%A9on_Bonnat "Léon Bonnat"). He returned to Paris in 1882.[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-19) Toulouse-Lautrec's mother had high ambitions and, with the aim of her son becoming a fashionable and respected painter, used their family's influence to gain him entry to Bonnat's studio.[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-TL-Book1-8) He was drawn to [Montmartre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montmartre "Montmartre"), the area of Paris known for its bohemian lifestyle and the haunt of artists, writers, and philosophers. Studying with Bonnat placed Toulouse-Lautrec in the heart of Montmartre, an area he rarely left over the next 20 years.

### Disability and health problems
After Bonnat took a new job, Toulouse-Lautrec moved to the studio of [Fernand Cormon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fernand_Cormon "Fernand Cormon") in 1882 and studied for a further five years and established the group of friends he kept for the rest of his life. At this time, he met [Émile Bernard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89mile_Bernard_(painter) "Émile Bernard (painter)") and [Vincent van Gogh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincent_van_Gogh "Vincent van Gogh"). Cormon, whose instruction was more relaxed than Bonnat's, allowed his pupils to roam Paris, looking for subjects to paint. During this period, Toulouse-Lautrec had his first encounter with a prostitute (reputedly sponsored by his friends), which led him to paint his first painting of a prostitute in [Montmartre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montmartre "Montmartre"), a woman rumoured to be Marie-Charlet.[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-TL-Book1-8)

Toulouse-Lautrec's family were [Anglophiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglophile "Anglophile"),[[32]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-32) and though he was not as fluent as he pretended to be, he spoke English well enough.[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-Ch4-TL-29) He travelled to London, where he was commissioned by the J. & E. Bella company to make a poster advertising their paper confetti (plaster confetti was banned after the 1892 [Mardi Gras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mardi_Gras "Mardi Gras"))[[33]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-33)[[34]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-34) and the bicycle advert _La Chaîne Simpson_.[[35]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-35)

### Interactions with women
While in London, Toulouse-Lautrec met and befriended [Oscar Wilde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar_Wilde "Oscar Wilde").[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-Ch4-TL-29) When Wilde faced imprisonment in Britain, Toulouse-Lautrec became a very vocal supporter of him, and his portrait of Oscar Wilde was painted the same year as Wilde's trial.[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-Ch4-TL-29)[[36]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-36)

Toulouse-Lautrec was mocked for his short stature and physical appearance, which some biographers theorise may have contributed to his alcoholism.[[37]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-info-37)

### Interactions with women
Initially, Toulouse-Lautrec only drank beer and wine, but his tastes expanded into spirits, namely [absinthe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absinthe "Absinthe").[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-wittels-25) The "Earthquake Cocktail" (_Tremblement de Terre_) is attributed to Toulouse-Lautrec: a potent mixture containing half absinthe and half [cognac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognac_(drink) "Cognac (drink)") in a wine goblet.[[38]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-38) Because of his underdeveloped legs, he walked with the aid of a cane, which he hollowed out and kept filled with liquor.[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-Ch4-TL-29)[[39]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-39)
Like Claude Monet, Lautrec was known for his cooking. Culinary historian Alexandra Leaf highlights Monet and Lautrec as the two "most serious gourmets" among their peers. Lautrec's love for cooking stemmed from his family upbringing and early childhood, where he first learned culinary values.[[40]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-leaf-40) Before he became widely known as a painter, he was called "Henri the cook" by his cousins.[[41]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-41)

### Interactions with women
Lautrec's penchant for art and cooking eventually led him to combine the visual and culinary arts, usually as a formal celebration of the release of new artworks, resulting in noteworthy parties for which he designed the menu (which were often artworks in themselves), bought and prepared the food, and created new cocktails. Lautrec's most famous party, held at the Natansons' Paris house in February 1895, is often regarded as one of the most notorious parties in art history. Three hundred guests were invited, with a claimed 2,000 cocktails served, all accompanied by side dishes of gourmet food, with Lautrec working diligently as the sole chef and bartender, dressed in a white linen jacket, complete with a freshly shaved bald head and no beard. Ironically, by the end of the night, Lautrec appeared to be the only one still awake and sober.[[42]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-42)[[40]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-leaf-40)

### Interactions with women
Lautrec was part of a group of gourmands, initially formed by [Gustave Geffroy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustave_Geffroy "Gustave Geffroy") (1855–1926), the historian of the Impressionists, and Claude Monet, who met up every Friday night for dinner at _[Drouant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drouant "Drouant")_, a restaurant in the [Palais Garnier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palais_Garnier "Palais Garnier") neighbourhood.[[43]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-mac-43) After Lautrec's death, his close friend Maurice Joyant published Lautrec's collection of recipes–some original, some adapted–in the book _La Cuisine de Monsieur Momo, Célibataire_ (1930), along with a colour [book frontispiece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_frontispiece "Book frontispiece") of _Toulouse Lautrec Cooking_ by Vuillard.[[43]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-mac-43)[[44]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-44) It was revised in 1966 as _L'art de la cuisine_. The [cookbook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cookbook "Cookbook") features 150 recipes, many of which were Lautrec's signature dishes, such as "Catalan salad". Art historian Charles Stuckey describes it as the first cookbook ever published by an artist, albeit posthumously.[[45]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-45) An English translation, _The Art of Cuisine_, was published in 1966.[[46]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-46)

### Interactions with women
[![Image 8](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e7/Verdelais_HTL_Tombe02.jpg/250px-Verdelais_HTL_Tombe02.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Verdelais_HTL_Tombe02.jpg)
Toulouse-Lautrec's grave in [Verdelais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verdelais "Verdelais") Cemetery
By February 1899, Toulouse-Lautrec's alcoholism began to take its toll, and he collapsed from exhaustion. His family had him committed to [Folie Saint James](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folie_Saint_James "Folie Saint James"), a sanatorium in [Neuilly-sur-Seine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuilly-sur-Seine "Neuilly-sur-Seine") for three months.[[47]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-47) While committed, he drew 39 circus portraits. After his release, he returned to the Paris studio and travelled throughout France.[[48]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-48) Both his physical and mental health began to decline due to alcoholism and [syphilis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syphilis "Syphilis").[[49]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-foundationbio-49)

### Interactions with women
In March 1901, a stroke left Lautrec paralysed from the legs down and confined him to a wheelchair. On 15 August 1901, whilst in Taussat, he suffered a stroke which left him [hemiplegic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemiplegic "Hemiplegic"). On 9 September 1901, at the age of 36, Toulouse-Lautrec died from complications due to alcoholism and syphilis at his mother's estate, [Château Malromé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%C3%A2teau_Malrom%C3%A9 "Château Malromé"), in [Saint-André-du-Bois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-Andr%C3%A9-du-Bois "Saint-André-du-Bois"). He is buried in [Verdelais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verdelais "Verdelais") Cemetery, Gironde, a few kilometres from the estate.[[49]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-foundationbio-49)[[50]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-bennett-50) Toulouse-Lautrec's last words reportedly were "Le vieux con!" ("The old fool!"), his goodbye to his father, who was killing a fly over his son's deathbed.[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-Ch4-TL-29)
After Toulouse-Lautrec's death, his mother, _[Comtesse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Count "Count")_ Adèle de Toulouse-Lautrec-Montfa, and his art dealer, Maurice Joyant, continued promoting his artwork. His mother contributed funds for a museum to be created in [Albi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albi "Albi"), his birthplace, to show his works. The [Musée Toulouse-Lautrec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mus%C3%A9e_Toulouse-Lautrec "Musée Toulouse-Lautrec") owns the most extensive collection of his works.

Toulouse-Lautrec's debt to the [Impressionists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impressionism "Impressionism"), particularly the more figurative painters like [Manet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89douard_Manet "Édouard Manet") and [Degas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgar_Degas "Edgar Degas"), is apparent, that within his works, one can draw parallels to the detached barmaid at _[A Bar at the Folies-Bergère](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A\_Bar\_at\_the\_Folies-Berg%C3%A8re "A Bar at the Folies-Bergère")_ by Manet and the behind-the-scenes ballet dancers of Degas. Toulouse-Lautrec's style was also influenced by the [ukiyo-e](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukiyo-e "Ukiyo-e") genre (Japanese woodblock prints), which became [popular in the Parisian art world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japonisme "Japonisme").[[52]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-52)

### Interactions with women
Toulouse-Lautrec excelled at depicting people in their working environments, with the colour and movement of the gaudy nightlife present but the glamour stripped away. He was a master at painting crowd scenes where each figure was highly individualised. At the time they were painted, the individual figures in his larger paintings could be identified by silhouette _alone_, and the names of many of these characters have been recorded.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation\_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_] His treatment of his subject matter, whether as portraits, in scenes of Parisian nightlife, or as intimate studies, has been described as alternately "sympathetic" and "dispassionate".[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation\_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
Toulouse-Lautrec's skilled depiction of people relied on his highly [linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_art "Line art") approach emphasising [contours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contour_line "Contour line"). He often applied paint in long, thin brushstrokes leaving much of the board visible. Many of his works may be best described as "drawings in coloured paint."[[53]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec#cite_note-53)

As promised in a previous blog post([Michelangelo’s Sisters: (Re)Introducing Female Old Masters](https://artherstory.net/rediscovering-female-old-masters/)) here is a post that highlights the names and works of twelve female Old Masters. These dozen names represent only a small percentage of the women who were not only successful artists in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, but were _famous_ for their art. Future posts will feature information about more women artists of the period, and samples of their work.

### Plautilla Nelli (1524**–**1588)
Born Pulisena Margherita Nelli, Suor Plautilla was a nun in Florence, and a follower of Savonarola. She taught herself to paint by copying other artists, notably Fra Bartolomeo. Her paintings—which include large-scale devotional works, wood lunettes, and miniatures—inspired the founding of [Advancing Women Artists](http://advancingwomenartists.org/home-1), AWA, which has ensured the restoration of some of her work. The restoration of Nelli’s 22-foot canvas depicting a Last Supper is due to be completed in 2019; see Jane Fortune’s 2017 [article](http://www.theflorentine.net/art-culture/2017/03/nelli-last-supper/) in _The Florentine_ about this painting. It is the only Last Supper known to be created by a Renaissance woman artist.
![Image 1](https://artherstory.net/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/Pained_Madonna.png)
_Pained Madonna_, 16th century, by Plautilla Nelli. The Museum of the Last Supper of Andrea del Sarto. Source: [Wikimedia](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Pained_Madonna.png).

### Catharina or Caterina van Hemessen (1528**–**after 1565)
Like many of early modernity’s women artists, Catharina van Hemessen was born into an artist family. Her father, Jan Sanders van Hemessen, was a prominent Mannerist painter in Antwerp. While she did paint a few religious-themed works, for the most part, Catharina van Hemessen was a portrait artist. She attracted the patronage of Maria of Austria; at Maria’s invitation, van Hemessen moved from the Netherlands to Spain. Upon Maria’s death, the painter was accorded a pension for life. The self-portrait shown here is the earliest known portrait of an artist (of either gender) at work at the easel.
![Image 2](https://artherstory.net/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/1920px-Hemessen-Selbstbildnis-1-781x1024.jpg)

### Sofonisba Anguissola (1532**–**1625)
Born into an aristocratic family in Cremona, Italy, the oldest of seven children, Sofonisba Anguissola was an artistic prodigy. In her lifetime, her talent was noted by Michelangelo, Vasari, and Van Dyck. Unusually for a woman, she was permitted to apprentice as a painter. As a young woman, she spent some years in the court of Philip II as a lady-in-waiting (during which service she was able to continue painting). Her surviving works, which are held in museums all over the world, include at least sixteen self-portraits, from all stages of her life—from teenager to old woman. According to the [Museum of Fine Arts, Boston website](https://www.mfa.org/collections/object/self-portrait-33656), she “executed more self-portraits than any other artist in the period between Dürer and Rembrandt.”

### Lavinia Fontana (1552**–**1614)
A Mannerist painter from Bologna, Italy, Fontana lays claim to a number of “firsts.” She is credited with being the first professional female artist. She was the first woman artist to paint female nudes, and possibly the first female to use live nude female models. She was the primary breadwinner for herself, her husband (also an artist) and their eleven children. She painted portraits, often of women, and also religious and mythological scenes. Fontana was elected into the Accademia di San Luca in Rome, where she and her family had moved at the invitation of Pope Clement VIII. She received numerous honors in her lifetime. As just one example, in 1611 sculptor and architect Felice Antonio Casoni cast a [bronze portrait medallion](https://www.nga.gov/collection/art-object-page.45169.html) of this artist! Read more about Fontana in Liz Lev’s [Art Herstory guest post](https://artherstory.net/lavinia-fontana-italys-first-female-professional-artist/).

### Artemisia Gentileschi (1593**–**after 1654)
Like her painter father Orazio Gentileschi, Artemisia Gentileschi was one of the Caravaggisti, followers of Caravaggio. She was born in Rome, where she was trained. Over the course of her career, she also worked in (at least) Florence, Naples, Venice and England. She attracted an international clientele, including members of the Medici house, and Charles I of England. Early in her career, she survived not only the trauma of abuse at the hands of a teacher, but also the trauma of the subsequent public trial. She specialized in painting women as allegory, and pictures of strong and suffering women from myths and the Bible.

### Giovanna Garzoni (1600**–**1670)
Today, Garzoni is best known for her delicate watercolor paintings of plants, vegetables, and animals. But she also painted religious, mythological, and allegorical subjects, as well as portraits. She is thought to have been born in Ascoli Piceno, Italy. If she married, the marriage was short-lived and ended in annulment. She is not known to have had children. She traveled and worked all over present-day Italy (including Venice, Naples, Rome, Turin and Florence) and in Paris. She left her estate to the Church of Santa Martina, the church of the Accademia di San Luca, in the understanding that she would be buried in that church.

### Judith Leyster (1609**–**1660)
Leyster, one of the first women to be admitted to the Saint Luke’s Guild of Haarlem, was a Dutch Golden Age painter of still lifes, portraits, and genre scenes. For almost 200 years after her death, experts attributed her works either to Frans Hals or to her husband, artist Jan Miense Molenaer. Many were re-attributed to her upon the discovery of her signature, a distinctive monogram of her initials which incorporates a shooting star—a play on words with her name, since Leyster means “lode star” in Dutch. Of her surviving works, she painted most in her young adulthood, before she married. But a self-portrait she painted later in life has recently come to light.

### Louise Moillon (1610**–**1696)
Born into a family of artists—her father and her stepfather were painters and art dealers, and her brother was a painter—Louise Moillon specialized in still life painting, especially of fruits and vegetables. Her patrons included members of the French nobility, as well as Charles I of England. Most of the forty or so works attributed to her today are from before 1640, though a few are from the 1670s.

### Mary Beale (c. 1633**–**1699)
Mary Beale, née Cradock, was one of the first professional female artists in England. She supported her family with her commissions and as an art teacher. Primarily a portrait artist, Beale painted many of the courtiers of Charles II. Her husband Charles acted as her business partner. His detailed notebooks provide a unique record of his wife’s daily activities, as well as of her business practices and expenses. Beale herself is author of _Observations_, an unpublished piece of instructional writing on painting.

### Elisabetta Sirani (1638**–**1665)
By the time of her death at the age of only 27, Sirani was one of the most famous painters in Bologna. A former maid servant was tried, but acquitted, for having poisoning the artist. Historians now speculate that Sirani died of peritonitis, in the wake of a ruptured peptic ulcer. Her portraits, mythological subjects, and especially her images of the Holy Family and the Virgin and Child, gained her international fame. In addition to her artistic achievements, she founded an academy for women painters in Bologna.
