Abstract 

In this work, the hydrodesulfurization (HDS) of 4,6-dimethyldibenzothiophene (4,6-DMDBT) was studied over three commercial generations of CoMoP/Al 2O3 catalysts in a fixed bed reactor under conditions close to those commonly used in diesel fuel hydrotreating (290 to 320 ºC and total pressure of 30 bar). 4,6-DMDBT is known to be transformed through two pathways: hydrogenation (HYD) and direct  desulfurization (DDS) .

Preliminary catalytic results obtained under typical operating conditions did not enable to discriminate the difference of reactivity of the three catalysts for DDS of 4,6-DMDBT. Therefore the operating conditions were changed in order to have discrimination on the DDS pathway. The parameter that seems to be more appropriate was the H 2S partial pressure, controlled by the quantity of dimethyldisulfide (DMDS) introduced in the liquid feed. Indeed an increase in H 2S partial pressure allowed to set up discriminatory conditions, since 1,2 %wt of DMDS led to coherent results with those obtained on real feedstock. 

The three generations of catalysts have different catalytic performances and the most recent generation of catalyst (CoMo-3) revealed to be the most active. It was observed that the HDS activity of 4,6-DMDBT is enhanced from generation to generation by an increasing of the HYD yield. 

Finally, the study of the influence of H 2S partial pressure showed very different behaviours between catalysts. CoMo-1 and CoMo-2 were negative impacted (more in the first case), whereas CoMo-3 was hardly affected by H 2S in the studied range. 

The optimized HDS test is now suitable to screen CoMo catalysts, with a high repeatability and allowing a rapid answer on the transformation of refractory sulfide molecules. 

Keywords: Diesel, deep hydrodesulfurization, 4,6-dimethyldibenzothiophene, CoMoP/Al 2O3,H2S partial pressure. 3

Resumo 

Neste trabalho, estudou-se a hidrodessulfurização (HDS) de 4,6-dimetildibenzotiofeno (4,6-DMDBT) em três gerações de catalisadores comerciais CoMoP/Al 2O3, num reactor de leito fixo em condições tipicamente utilizadas no hidrotratamento do gasóleo (290 a 320 ºC e uma pressão total de 30 bar). A transformação do 4,6-DMDBT ocorre através de duas vias reaccionais: a via de hidrogenação (HYD) e a via de dessulfurização directa (DSD). 

Os resultados dos testes catalíticos preliminares obtidos em condições típicas operacionais, não permitiram discriminar as diferentes reactividades dos três catalisadores pela via DSD da transformação do 4,6-DMDBT. Neste sentido, as condições operatórias foram modificadas para diferenciar a reactividade dos catalisadores dessa via. A pressão parcial de H 2S foi o parâmetro mais adequado a ser modificado, sendo controlada pela quantidade de dimetildissulfureto (DMDS) introduzida na alimentação líquida. De facto, um aumento da pressão parcial de H 2S permitiu definir as condições desejadas, uma vez que, os resultados obtidos a 1,2 %p/p (percentagem em peso) de DMDS foram coerentes com os resultados obtidos usando carga real (gasóleo industrial não tratado). 

As três gerações de catalisadores têm diferentes actividades catalíticas, sendo a geração mais recente (CoMo-3) aquela que apresenta a actividade catalítica mais elevada. De geração em geração, observou-se que a melhoria de HDS de 4,6-DMDBT é devida ao aumento do rendimento da via de hidrogenação. 

Finalmente, o estudo do impacto da pressão parcial de H 2S mostrou que os catalisadores têm comportamentos distintos. O catalisador CoMo-1 e CoMo-2 foram negativamente afectados (mais no primeiro caso), enquanto o CoMo-3 não foi praticamente afectado no intervalo de pressão parcial de H2S estudado. 

O teste de HDS optimizado é agora adequado para proceder à escolha de catalisadores CoMo, com uma elevada repetibilidade e com uma resposta rápida na reacção de transformação de moléculas de enxofre refractárias.

# 1.  Introduction 

The sulfur content in diesel fuel has been drastically reduced over the years by restrictive regulations adopted by the European Community, in order to decrease the pollution caused by motor vehicles. In addition to the issues related to the legislation, the refining industry is processing heavier feeds and facing an increasing demand of diesel fuel, so the need of more efficient catalysts used in hydrodesulfurization (HDS) is being required. The most common commercial catalysts used for HDS are molybdenum sulfides promoted by cobalt or nickel supported over alumina. 

In HDS of diesel, as sulfur conversion increases the remaining species are mostly dibenzothiophenes with alkyl substituents in the 4 and 6 positions on the aromatic rings adjacent to the heterocycle, such as 4,6-dimethyldibenzothiophene (4,6-DMDBT). These species are the most refractory compounds, because they are very difficult to eliminate. The conversion of these compounds proceeds by two distinct pathways: hydrogenation (HYD) and direct desulfurization (DDS). 

It was observed that HDS of dibenzothiophene (DBT) is mainly conducted by DDS pathway, whereas for 4,6-DMDBT it is mainly converted by HYD pathway. The presence of the methyl groups on 4,6-DMDBT highly reduces the reactivity and preferentially inhibits the DDS pathway. 

However, DDS is the pathway which consumes less hydrogen so there is a high interest in obtaining catalysts with increased DDS. To do so, it is very important to evaluate them by a HDS screening test, at the closest conditions to the real application. 

Therefore, this work will focus on the optimization of a HDS test in the presence of 4,6-DMDBT over CoMo catalysts in fixed bed reactor, in order to: 

i. find out the suitable conditions for the prevision of catalytic performances of the future catalyst generations, 

ii. understand the impact of those conditions on 4,6-DMDBT conversion. 

Three different commercial generations of CoMo catalysts will be studied by following the 4,6-DMDBT conversion, HYD and DDS pathway at the different operating conditions, particularly by varying the partial pressure of H 2S, to evaluate the potential of discrimination of the test. 

In a first part of this work the advances made in this field are summarized. Then, the second part comprises the description of the experimental work. The third part presents the catalytic results and their discussion. Finally, the major conclusions and some perspectives of the present work are presented. 11 12

# 2.1. Context 

In the last years humanity has been facing a tremendous evolution in the consumption of energy, due to the economic growth and the expanding populations in the world’s developing countries [1] .

As a matter of fact, the petroleum plays an essential role in order to fulfil this increasing demand, with special regard to the transportation fuels. A remarkable evidence indicates that from 2000 to 2008 the global consumption of diesel fuel has increased by 23% [2] .

Concerning the environment and the health of population the aim is to produce cleaner liquid hydrocarbon fuels. For diesel fuels one of the most concerning issue is the sulfur content. During the combustion of diesel fuel the sulfur compounds are converted into SO x which can react with vapor water present in the atmosphere, producing sulfuric acid (known as acid rain): 

2SO 2 + O 2 → 2SO 3

SO 3+ H 2O → H 2SO 4

Moreover, sulfur compounds are responsible for poisoning the catalytic converters of exhaust emission used in vehicles [3] . This is also true for refinery plants where sulfur-containing feeds can poison the catalysts used in their processes (e.g. FCC). 

Besides, particulate matter are formed through condensation and nucleation of semivolatile species (such as H 2SO 4) by cooling the combustion gases on the exhaust pipe vehicles, with special regard to inhalable particles with diameter lower than 10 μm (known as PM10) which may cause serious injuries to human health at long-term exposure [4,5] .

Consequently, the solution arises in catalysis by means of catalytic hydrotreatment process, particularly the so called hydrodesulfurization (HDS). 

However, over time due to the deeper extraction of crude oil, the sulfur content is becoming higher and if we take into account that regulatory specifications for diesel fuels can be even more strengthened, the refineries are confronted to a huge challenge of deep or even ultra-deep HDS. 

New design approaches are already being developed to meet this challenge both in process technology and in catalysts performance [6] . Catalysis research is a very attractive approach, since obtaining catalysts with higher activity is desirable to meet the specifications for liquid fuels, thus avoiding the increase in the consumption of energy and raw material. 13 

# 2.2.  Generalities  of Hydrotreatment 

Hydrotreaters are very important units in a petroleum refinery, since they allow reaching the specifications required for the final products (e.g. liquid fuels). Crude oil always contains impurities (mostly in the organic form) and the function of these units is to remove the undesirable heteroatoms contained therein ( Table 1 ).

The exploration of heavy and extra-heavy crude oil is nowadays a general tendency and leads to an increasing amount of impurities, majority sulfur and nitrogen compounds. Impurities concerning metals are mostly nickel and vanadium and they are presented as big structures with numerous heterocyclic aromatic rings, which are known as asphaltenes. 

The hydrotreatment process takes place in a reactor with a catalytic fixed-bed in the presence of high-pressure of hydrogen and the feed to be treated.  The t ypical  operating  cond itions for diesel 

hydrotreatment  comprise reaction temperature between 280 to 340 °C and hydrogen pres sure 

between 25 to 55 bar  [8].

During the process the feed is combined with hydrogen, heated to the reactor inlet temperature and then pass through the catalyst bed. The reactions of hydrogenation are exothermic so the temperature of the reactor might be controlled by hydrogen quenching [9] . Usually, hydrotreatment reactors could have more than one catalyst bed depending on the content of impurities in the feed, configuration system and operation conditions (e.g. hydrogen pressure). The role of the hydrotreatment reactors depends on the feed to be treated and the future use of the effluent (diesel pool, pretreatment before units such as catalytic reforming, FCC,...). They can perform several reactions to remove the impurities from crude oil. The choice of the catalyst is thus crucial. The hydrotreatment used to produce clean liquid fuels such as diesel should meet the required specifications, so the catalysts used therein may provide  hydrodesulfurization (HDS) ,

hydrodenitrogenation (HDN) and  hydro dearomatization  (HDA) reactions, since they are the main

# 2.3. Diesel Fuel: Characteristics and chemical composition 

Since the present study is focused on hydrotreatment of diesel, this part is presenting the diesel properties. 

Concerns about the quality of air have been increasing and the main measures taken were pointed directly to the composition of liquid fuels. For this reason the composition of diesel fuel is defined according to the legislation and to the characteristics of diesel engines. The specifications imposed by EU directives for diesel fuel are shown in  Table 2 . 

> Table 2- Diesel specifications for vehicles equipped with compression ignition engines for EU [11]

Diesel fuel can be essentially obtained by two sources from oil refining, which are: middle distillates from atmospheric distillation (BP: 250 to 350 °C) and Vacuum Gas Oil (BP: 340 to 550 °C) from vacuum distillation [10 ]. Depending on the configuration, some refineries can also use light cycle oil (LCO) from FCC or gas oil from visbreaker as other sources. 

Concerning the chemistry of diesel fuel, its range comprises hydrocarbon molecules with a number of carbon atoms between C 13 to C 25 .

In diesel engines it is desired that fuel self-combust when it is compressed and this tendency is measured by the cetane number, which by definition is 100 for hexadecane (or cetane) and 0 for  α-16 

methylnaphathalene. Higher is the chain length and linearity of hydrocarbon molecules, higher is the cetane number and easier is the combustion of diesel fuel. Contrary to this, aromatic compounds tend to resist to self-ignition, so a diesel fuel with considerable amounts of them is not desirable. In addition, their quantity is limited because they are carcinogenic compounds and thus extremely harmful to human health. 

Overtime, the major change in diesel composition has been the sulfur content at 500 ppm in 1996 to 10 ppm in 2009 [12] , which corresponds to a reduction of 98%. The causes are strictly related to environmental concerns, as it has been previously discussed. 

More recently, the introduction of FAME (fatty acid methyl esther) in diesel fuels has been done in order to stimulate the production of liquid fuels from renewable sources, like the so known "biodiesel". Even so, the quantity of biodiesel should have a maximum limit due to the chemical aggressiveness towards rubber and metallic materials used in vehicles engines. 

Other additives are usually added to reach better properties depending on the purpose, such as additives to lower the freezing point of diesel which is critic in some countries where the temperatures can be negative in winter. 

# 2.4. HDS Catalysts 

The commercial catalysts used for hydrotreatment consists in a metal sulfide usually molybdenum promoted with nickel or cobalt, supported over an oxide carrier generally alumina. The supported catalyst must have a suitable size and shape in order to minimize both the pressure drop in the reactor and the diffusion limitations. Normally, the catalysts are formed by small pellets with a diameter between 0,8 to 2 mm and a lenght between 1 to 6 mm. The aim is to obtain a catalyst with a high surface-to-volume ratio and this can be obtained by changing the pellets shape ( Figure 3 ). Nowadays, the cylinder, ring and trilobe shape are the most adopted for commercial hydrotreatment catalysts.  

> Figure 3- Typical shapes of catalytic particles [13]

The most used support is γ-alumina because it has good mechanical properties [14] . Besides γ-alumina with a high-surface area is also widely used since it allows a high dispersion of the active phase [15,16] .17 

The steps involved in the preparation of a metal sulfide catalyst are: impregnation, maturation, drying, calcination (optional) and activation. Commonly, phosphorus is added during impregnation to enhance the solubility of molybdenum by the formation of phosphomolybdate complexes [17] and the active phase dispersion. 

The activation step is also known as sulfidation and consists in transforming the oxidized metal form into the metal sulfide form, which is the active phase. The sulfidation can be done with a gas mixture of H 2S/H 2, with sulfiding agents like dimethyldisulfide or directly by the diesel feed at temperatures above 300 ºC. Generally, the sulfidation in industry is made in-situ by doing apresulfidation with the untreated diesel (rich in sulfur compounds) and then ending with a liquid feed of dimethyldisulfide. 

## 2.4.1. Unpromoted catalysts: MoS 2/Al 2O3

In the 1970s the majority of the hydrotreating catalysts were small particles of molybdenum sulfide (MoS 2) supported in the pores of γ-Al 2O3 and it was only later (in the 1980s) that discussion about the role of Ni and Co as promoters emerged [17 ].

Kasztelan et al. [18] refers that the active phase is made of well dispersed crystallites of MoS 2

in single slabs on the alumina surface. Each slab is made of an atomic structure of S-Mo-S, where molybdenum ions (Mo 2+ ) are coordinated with six sulfur ions (S 2-) in a trigonal prismatic configuration. Moreover, these slabs interacts with each other by weak Van der Waals forces, forming a layered structure with intercalated molybdenum "sandwiched" between two layers of sulfur (see  Figure 4 ).

The resulting layered structure could have a hexagonal shape, with two basal planes of sulfur and exhibits two types of edges: "Mo-edges" and "S-edges" as shown on  Figure 5 . It is known that the basal plane of sulfur is inert since the reacting molecules do not interfere with it. 18

These vacancies lead to the exposure of Mo cations, known as coordinatively unsaturated sites (CUS). ## 2.4.2. Promoted catalysts: CoMo/Al 2O3

When cobalt or nickel is added to the catalyst, the resulting sulfided Co(Ni)Mo/Al 2O3 solids 

have a substantially higher activity than Mo/Al 2O3. For this reason, Co and Ni are considered to act as promoters of MoS 2 activity [17 ].

The promoted CoMo catalysts supported on alumina will be described in more details, due to the focus of this study. 

Overtime, several authors [18 ,27] have proposed different models to describe the structure of these promoted catalysts in order to explain this enhanced activity. Among them, Topsoe et al. [27 ]

suggested the mixed phase "CoMoS" model, which is currently the widely accepted model. 

The combination of Mössbauer emission spectroscopy (MES) with measurements of HDS activity, allowed to find that the promotion effect is due to the cobalt ions in the Co-Mo-S structure, with promoter atoms being located at the MoS 2 edges [17 ,27 ].

As it can be seen on  Figure 8 , apart from the CoMoS phase, cobalt and molybdenum species are distributed in different structures at the catalyst surface. During sulfidation step, a part of cobalt atoms react with sulfur forming thermodynamically stable Co 9S8 crystallites (Co monosulfide phase). 

Another part of the cobalt atoms can  occupy tetrahedral sites inside the al umina support with 

a strong interaction (Co:Al 2O3). The calcination temperature influences greatly this  phenomenon. 20 

Molybdenum can also form stable crystals (molybdate species) disposed on the catalyst surface. 

Since, either Co 9S8 crystallites,  Co:Al 2O3 and molybdate species  are not catalytically active , it 

is intended to avoid their formation and maximize t he formation of Co -Mo -S structures by controlling 

the parameters  during the catalyst preparation  in order to obtain the most efficient solids .

As Co loading increases, a point will be reached when all edge positions are occupied and the Co atoms will start to form  Co 9S8 crystallites, so for high Co:Mo

It was observed that Co atoms have a huge impact on the morphology of the MoS 2 structures, because the resulting structures have a hexagonally truncated shape. This impact is driven by the incorporation of Co atoms into the S-edges of MoS 2 particles, leading to the formation of the so known Co-Mo-S phase. Indeed, cobalt addition stabilizes the S-edge relative to the situation of pure MoS 2[30 ,31] .

The promoted catalysts also adsorb H species on their edges forming SH groups. These groups have a special role, because the close vicinity of the co-adsorbed H atoms can favour hydrogenation, which is an attractive catalyst property. Therefore, it was observed that H atoms are strongly adsorbed on S-edges being less reactive, so it is expected that they are more available in Mo-edges for hydrogenation reactions [15 ].

Regarding the role of the promoter, it is believed that the substitution of Mo by Co atoms at S-edges will enhance the formation of sulfur vacancies and create new and more active sites [15 ].

One notion that is well established is related to the fact that Co-S bond is weaker than the Mo-S bond [30 ], so vacancy formation is expected to be easier. Also, it is known that Co increases the electronic density on the sulfur atoms, enhancing the basicity of specific S -2 centers important to HDS reactions [30 ,32] .

Recent studies [15 ,26 ] suggest another notion that the catalytic activity is not only due to the sulfur vacancies. Indeed, it was observed the existence of a new type of "brim" sites with a metallic-like character, which are located on the basal S atom adjacent to the edges and they may bind sulfur compounds without needing sulfur vacancies. These brims are the result of one or more metallic edge states and they can be identified by STM ( Figure 10  - Right hand side) as a very bright brim extending along the edges. More specifically,  it was seen that Mo -edges presents a special edge state and 

possesses an activity towards hydrogenation  [15 ].22 

However these sites have been evidenced in STM conditions, far from HDS operating conditions (model gold support, ultra vacuum conditions). 

These observations highlight the complexity of catalysis by sulfides to precisely describe the active site. 

## 2.4.3. Support interaction in CoMo/Al 2O3 catalysts 

The support interacts with the active phase and therefore influences the catalyst activity. In the literature, two different types of Co-Mo-S structures have been highlighted and have been termed “Type I” and “Type II”. They actually differ from each other by support interactions. 

In the so called "Type I" the active phase is connected to the support by Mo-O-Al linkages, so this type is known to have a strong interaction with the support. In the other case, "Type II" is referred to have weak interaction with the support, with multilayer Co-Mo-S structures [15 ,16 ,17 ].

It has been shown that catalysts with weaker interactions present a higher activity rather than active phase with strong support interactions where the active phase is linked to the support. 

Weaker interactions can be obtained by changing the nature of the support, the sulfidation temperature or the use of additives [15 ].

During hydrotreatment process, the reactions take place in the liquid phase since most of the molecules do not vaporize at reactor pressure. Considering the numerous sulfur compounds available in petroleum, it is quite obvious that they will not have the same reactivity, as illustrated on  Figure 13 . Thiols, sulfides and disulfides are easier to be converted when compared to the heterocyclic compounds, whereas for the latter, the reactivity decreases with the increasing of aromatic ring number: tiophenes > benzotiophenes > dibenzotiophenes. In another way, longer is the molecular chain and higher is the number of aromatic rings, higher is the boiling point and lower is the reactivity (valid up to three aromatic rings [35] ). Song et al . [36 ] highlighted that when the sulfur content in diesel is reduced to 30 ppm, the remaining compounds are essentially alkyl dibenzothiophenes (DBTs) with alkyl groups at 4 or/and 6 positions (e.g. 4,6-dimethyldibenzothiophene). These compounds are termed as “refractory 24 

compounds” because they have a low HDS reactivity. This is in accordance with Whitehurst et al. [37]

The low reactivity of 4,6-DMDBT is explained by the steric hindrance caused by the methyl groups and also by the electronic factors around the sulfur atom [14 ,35 ].

Ultra-deep HDS of diesel fuel is thus a huge challenge since lower is the sulfur content, more difficult is the HDS. Therefore it becomes important to improve the catalysts activity towards refractory compounds such as 4,6-DMDBT. 

Organic nitrogen compounds can also be found in diesel, such as: indoles, carbazoles, quinolines, acridines and phenanthridines [14 ]:

# 2.6. Conversion of refractory compounds 

Knowledge in the field of sulfur removal of refractory compounds has been systematically improved and thus some fundamental notions are now well established. It is known that HDS of DBT-type compounds is carried out by two parallel pathways: desulfurization through hydrogenation (HYD) and direct desulfurization (DDS) (or formerly known as direct hydrogenolysis) [14 ,32 ].

The HYD pathway is conducted via several hydrogenation reactions prior to sulfur removal, producing cyclohexylbenzene-type compounds, whereas DDS pathway goes through the direct elimination of sulfur, producing biphenyl-type compounds. The overall reaction scheme for the conversion of 4,6-DMDBT is shown on  Figure 15 .25

The final products obtained from HYD pathway are methylcyclohexyltoluene ( MCHT ) and dimethyldicyclohexyl (DMDCH ). From DDS pathway the final products are C14 H14 S and dimethylbiphenyl ( DMBPh ). It should be noted, that there are several intermediate products that came from 4,6-DMDBT conversion but they are not observed because they are very reactive. 

As it can be seen, HYD pathway consumes more hydrogen than the DDS pathway, so it is interesting to obtain catalysts with a high DDS selectivity. 

## 2.6.1. Reaction mechanisms 

Bataille et al. [32 ] have proposed the mechanism for the pathways of 4,6-DMDBT conversion. They assumed that the first step to take place is the partial hydrogenation of 4,6-DMDBT to form a dihydrointermediate (see scheme of  Figure 15 ), and this step is expected to be the most difficult since it destroys the aromaticity of the benzenic ring. Nine isomers of dihydrointermediates might be formed, performed by 1,2 or 1,4 addition of two hydrogen atoms. However, compounds formed by 1,4 addition are unstable because the double bonds present in their aromatic ring are not conjugated (i.e. 

electrons cannot be delocalized over the electronic system) [32 ].

The scheme presented on  Figure 15  is still oversimplified, so each pathway can be more detailed. In the case of HYD pathway, the different steps reported on  Figure 16  are described below. 26

1- Subsequent hydrogenation of the dihydroisomers, forming tetrahydroisomers; 

2- Elimination reaction (E 2) leading to the first C-S bond cleavage;

4- Elimination reaction (E 2) performing the second C-S bond cleavage, thus forming MCHT; 

5- Further hydrogenation of MCHT can occur, leading to the production of DMDCH.

# 2.7. Objective of the study 

Nowadays, new catalysts with better performances are constantly designed to meet the several requirements on diesel specification claimed by oil industry. As a consequence, in R&D laboratories, it is very important to find out which are the catalysts with better activity by performing a screening. This evaluation is made by catalytic tests at the closest conditions to the real application. 

The sulfur compounds encountered in diesel feeds are of several types. However upon hydrotreatment, it only remains the most refractory molecules, such as alkyl-dibenzothiophenes. These molecules are known to react according two distinct pathways, hydrogenation (HYD) and direct desulfurization (DDS). To better understand the catalytic properties it is now becoming essential to develop a catalytic test allowing to discriminate the catalysts on the DDS route. Indeed finding new catalyst formulations with increased DDS would allow to decrease considerably the hydrogen consumption. Catalytic test on real feedstock exist, but they take a relative long time (e.g. 3 weeks) being  disadvantageous in several ways such  as  the consumption of raw material and waiting time to 

validate (or not) the tested catalyst s. Aware of this,  it  became necessary to  develop a new catalytic 

test on  model molecules, which  can provide  a rapid answer on the transformation of refractory sulfide 

molecules. 

Such a test has been set up in 2009-2010 at IFPEN on a micro fixed bed reactor (4 cm 3 of catalyst), allowing the test of solids in the conversion of 4,6-dimethyldibenzothiophene (4,6-DMDBT). 

The objective of the present study was first to optimise this test in order to find out the suitable operating conditions allowing to discriminate three generations of known CoMoP catalysts on the DDS route. In a second step the objective was to understand the impact of these conditions on the 4,6-DMDBT conversion. 32

## 3.2.1. Equipment description – T033 unit 

The HDS tests were performed on the T033 unit. The HDS test allows to evaluate the catalysts performance in a fixed bed reactor, under hydrogen pressure, close to the industrial HDS conditions. The T033 unit scheme is given on  Figure 24 . 

> Figure 24-Scheme of the unit T033

- Liquid and gas feed:

The reactor outlet line is thermally insulated and heated at 280 ºC, keeping the reactor effluent in the gas phase and avoiding condensation of high boiling point compounds. This line is regulated by a thermocouple (TIC04) and has also a safety thermocouple.

# 4.6. Conclusion 

The preliminary catalytic results obtained were in agreement with the former catalytic results related to the 4,6-DMDBT conversion and HYD pathway, whereas for the DDS pathway there was no discrimination with the initial chosen conditions. 

In order to obtain a discrimination between the catalysts on the DDS pathway, the operating conditions had to be modified. The parameter that seems to be the more pertinent was the H 2S partial pressure. 

The H 2S partial pressure was controlled by the quantity of DMDS introduced in the liquid feed. Therefore it was increased from 0.8 %wt of DMDS (initial conditions pH 2S = 0,22 bar) to 1,4 %wt of DMDS (pH 2S = 0,38 bar). At 1,2 %wt of DMDS (pH 2S = 0,32 bar) it was found a good discrimination between the catalysts and the results are very consistent with the HDS test on real feedstock. 

For these chosen conditions, the catalytic performances increased in the following sense: CoMo-1<CoMo-2<CoMo-3, whatever the temperature and the pathway considered. However the CoMo-1 catalyst was the less active but the more selective on DDS pathway. 

The increase on the overall HDS activity of 4,6-DMDBT through catalysts generation is actually achieved by an increasing of the HYD pathway. The results from toluene hydrogenation confirmed the higher hydrogenation capacity of the most recent generations, such as CoMo-3 catalyst. 

The apparent activation energy values are similar for the three solids, around 50 kcal/mol which seems to indicate that the 4,6-DMDBT conversion mechanism is the same for the three catalysts. 

Finally, the study of the influence of H 2S partial pressure showed that the three catalysts have very different behaviours. CoMo-1 and CoMo-2 were negative impacted on HYD and DDS pathways reflecting a decrease in the catalytic activity (more in the first case), whereas CoMo-3 was hardly affected by H 2S in the studied range. However, a slight promotion effect was observed at 290 ºC, but this should be confirmed by further experiments. 65 66 

# 5.  Conclusion and Perspectives 

The main objective of this work was to optimise the conditions of an existing HDS test in a fixed bed reactor, in the presence of 4,6-DMDBT for the future screening of CoMo sulfide catalysts used in diesel HDS. 

Three generations of commercial catalysts were evaluated by following the 4,6-DMDBT conversion, HYD and DDS pathways. The preliminary catalytic results obtained were in agreement with the former catalytic results related to the 4,6-DMDBT conversion and HYD pathway, whereas for the DDS pathway there was no discrimination with the initial chosen conditions. 

Therefore, the operating conditions were changed in order to have discrimination between the catalysts on the DDS pathway. The parameter that seems to be more appropriate was the H 2S partial pressure, controlled by the quantity of DMDS introduced in the liquid feed. Indeed, an increase in H 2Spartial pressure allowed to set up discriminatory conditions, since 1,2 %wt of DMDS added to the feed led to coherent results with those obtained on real feedstock. 

The catalytic performances increased in the order of: CoMo-1 < CoMo-2 < CoMo-3 and it was evidenced that the HDS activity of 4,6-DMDBT is enhanced from generation to generation by an increasing of the HYD pathway. 

Finally, the study of the influence of H 2S partial pressure showed that the three catalysts have very different behaviours. CoMo-1 and CoMo-2 were negative impacted on HYD and DDS pathways reflecting a decrease in the catalytic activity (more on the first case), whereas CoMo-3 was hardly affected by H 2S in the studied range. However, a slight promotion effect was observed, but this should be confirmed by further experiments. 

This could be done by decreasing the contact time (i.e. increasing LHSV) or increasing the H2S partial pressure. Moreover, the kinetic order with respect to 4,6-DMDBT could be determined by varying the LHSV for a given temperature, in order to confirm the hypothesis based on the literature of first-order reaction, particularly for new catalyst generation. 

The optimized HDS test is suitable to screen CoMo catalysts, with a high repeatability and allowing a rapid answer  on the transformation of refractory sulfide molecules (1 day test). Furthermore, 

this test also provides a better understanding on the catalytic performances of different catalysts 

generations and will be definite ly useful for the development of new highly active catalysts. 67 68
