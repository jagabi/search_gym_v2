Socrates, an Athenian philosopher who lived from 470/469 to 399 BCE, is considered one of the most important figures in the development of philosophical thought, despite never having written any of his teachings. Plato, a student of Socrates who lived from 427 to 347 BCE, is our primary source of knowledge about Socrates, having documented his mentor's ideas and dialogues in his works.

Plato's philosophical journey was significantly influenced by Socrates. Initially interested in politics, Plato's direction changed after Socrates' trial and execution in 399 BCE, leading him to focus on the question of what constitutes a virtuous life. This shift is evident in Plato's writings, which are largely dedicated to exploring Socratic ideas and methods. Plato's works are divided into three periods: the early "Socratic" dialogues, which closely follow Socrates' teachings; the "Middle" dialogues, where Plato begins to develop his own ideas; and the "Later" dialogues, reflecting his mature thought.

Socrates' influence on Plato was profound. Socrates introduced the Socratic method, a form of cooperative argumentative dialogue to stimulate critical thinking and to illuminate ideas. This method deeply impacted Plato, who used it as a foundation for his own philosophical inquiries. Despite this, there were notable differences in their philosophies. For instance, while Socrates focused primarily on ethics and epistemology, Plato's interests were broader, encompassing metaphysics, epistemology, ethics, and politics. Plato also developed the theory of Forms, which posits that non-physical Forms (or Ideas) represent the most accurate reality, a concept that goes beyond Socrates' more practical approach to philosophical questions.
