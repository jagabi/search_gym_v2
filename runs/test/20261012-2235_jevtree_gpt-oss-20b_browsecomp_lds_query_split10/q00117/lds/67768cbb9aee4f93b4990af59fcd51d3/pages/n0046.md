# [Internet Archive to Demo Wayback Machine at Friday’s Uniqlo Street Festival](https://blog.archive.org/2026/10/08/internet-archive-to-demo-wayback-machine-at-fridays-uniqlo-street-festival/)

 Posted on [October 8, 2026](https://blog.archive.org/2026/10/08/internet-archive-to-demo-wayback-machine-at-fridays-uniqlo-street-festival/ "11:50 am") by [Chris Freeland](https://blog.archive.org/author/chrisfreeland/ "View all posts by Chris Freeland")

The Internet Archive is joining other area nonprofits for the [Uniqlo Street Festival](https://uniqlo.streetfest.us/) on Friday, October 9, 2026, alongside the opening of Uniqlo’s new flagship store on Market Street. We’ll be set up with fellow San Francisco nonprofits Gray Area and the Exploratorium to help bring people and energy back to a part of the city that has sorely missed shoppers and foot traffic. We’ll be demoing the Wayback Machine, handing out stickers, and talking with attendees about digital preservation.

“Libraries have always found interesting ways to get in front of new audiences. More people than ever need to know about the valuable service of the Wayback Machine and why our digital culture should be preserved. People may be aware of the Internet Archive, but may not realize that we are also a San Francisco organization. This gives us an opportunity to meet with people in our community during this event,” said Merrilee Proffitt, director of Democracy’s Library US at the Internet Archive, who will be at the booth on Friday. “Plus, to be honest, I think many of us have a Uniqlo hoodie or two in our closets.”

This isn’t an entirely new kind of partnership for libraries: earlier this year, the New York Public Library began a year-long partnership with Uniqlo supporting free cultural and youth programming, alongside the opening of Uniqlo’s Bryant Park store, as well as a [planned series of literary events](https://www.sfgate.com/local/article/new-san-francisco-uniqlo-22437348.php) around San Francisco hosted by the Friends of the San Francisco Public Library.

If you’d like to invite the Internet Archive to participate in your community street fair, reach out to our events coordinator at [info@archive.org](mailto:info@archive.org).

# [Internet Archive to Honor Poynter Institute and Investigative Reporters and Editors with 2026 Hero Award for Preserving Local News Online](https://blog.archive.org/2026/10/05/internet-archive-to-honor-poynter-institute-and-investigative-reporters-and-editors-with-2026-hero-award-for-preserving-local-news-online/)

 Posted on [October 5, 2026](https://blog.archive.org/2026/10/05/internet-archive-to-honor-poynter-institute-and-investigative-reporters-and-editors-with-2026-hero-award-for-preserving-local-news-online/ "12:00 pm") by [Chris Freeland](https://blog.archive.org/author/chrisfreeland/ "View all posts by Chris Freeland")

[![Image 2](https://blog.archive.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/HERO_AWARD_16_9.png)](https://blog.archive.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/HERO_AWARD_16_9.png)
**The Internet Archive is proud to announce that the Poynter Institute and Investigative Reporters and Editors (IRE) will receive the 2026 Internet Archive Hero Award for their leadership in preserving local news online.**

The Internet Archive Hero Award recognizes individuals and organizations that have demonstrated leadership in making information available to people around the world. The [Poynter Institute](https://www.poynter.org/), a 50-year-old nonprofit that works to strengthen journalism and train journalists, and [IRE](https://www.ire.org/), a member-driven nonprofit that has spent 50 years helping journalists do better investigative work, have made preservation a vital part of their work.

Local journalism is the first draft of history, and often the only record of what happened in a community. But as news increasingly moves online, that record is at risk. Stories can disappear when a newsroom closes, a website changes platforms, or digital archives are neglected. At the same time, major news publishers and media organizations are restricting access to web archiving tools like the [Wayback Machine](https://web.archive.org/), making it harder to preserve the journalism being published today.

Poynter and IRE are taking a different approach. In partnership with the Internet Archive, they are helping newsrooms understand the importance of digital preservation and giving journalists the tools and training they need to protect their work for the future.

Through the national initiative [_Today’s News for Tomorrow_](https://archive-it.org/programs/tnt/), the three organizations are working to train hundreds of newsrooms in digital preservation and provide access to Internet Archive’s web archiving and preservation services. The program recognizes that preserving local journalism cannot be left to chance: The people creating the news need to be part of preserving it.

The Internet Archive Hero Award is an annual award that honors those who have exhibited leadership in making information available for digital learners all over the world. Previous recipients have included Sir Tim Berners-Lee, the island nation of Aruba, public information advocate Carl Malamud, copyright expert Michelle Wu, and the Grateful Dead.

# [Vanishing Culture Episode #6: What We’ve Learned with Vida Vojić & Alice Bridgwood](https://blog.archive.org/2026/08/05/vanishing-culture-episode-6-what-weve-learned-with-vida-vojic-alice-bridgwood/)

 Posted on [August 5, 2026](https://blog.archive.org/2026/08/05/vanishing-culture-episode-6-what-weve-learned-with-vida-vojic-alice-bridgwood/ "12:00 pm") by [Chris Freeland](https://blog.archive.org/author/chrisfreeland/ "View all posts by Chris Freeland")

[![Image 6](https://blog.archive.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/FKP-Vanishing-Culture-What-Weve-Learned1080.png)](https://futureknowledge.transistor.fm/episodes/vanishing-culture-6-what-weve-learned-with-vida-vojic-alice-bridgwood)
What does it mean to preserve culture in an age of disappearing websites, platform-controlled media, and fragile digital memories? In the final episode of our special six-part series on _Vanishing Culture_, Future Knowledge hosts Dave Hansen and Chris Freeland sit down with musician and series host Vida Vojić and producer Alice Bridgwood to reflect on the conversations, ideas, and lessons that emerged throughout the series.

**MORE INFO:**[https://futureknowledge.transistor.fm/episodes/vanishing-culture-6-what-weve-learned-with-vida-vojic-alice-bridgwood](https://futureknowledge.transistor.fm/episodes/vanishing-culture-6-what-weve-learned-with-vida-vojic-alice-bridgwood)

Looking back on discussions about cookbooks, queer archives, African folklore, Papiamento, and more, Vida and Alice explore the common threads connecting seemingly disparate forms of cultural preservation. They reflect on how culture is shaped not only by institutions, but by communities and individuals who choose what to remember, share, and pass on. The conversation examines the challenges of living in an “age of platforms,” the risks of relying on technology and corporations to steward cultural memory, and the growing movement toward community-driven preservation efforts. Along the way, the group discusses what surprised them most, how the series changed their own habits and perspectives, and why they remain hopeful that people everywhere can play an active role in keeping culture alive. Rather than ending on a note of loss, the episode offers a hopeful reminder that culture survives when people care enough to preserve, share, and reimagine it together.

# [Vanishing Culture Episode #5: A Language Worth Saving with Peter Scholing](https://blog.archive.org/2026/07/29/vanishing-culture-episode-5-a-language-worth-saving-with-peter-scholing/)

[![Image 8](https://blog.archive.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/FKP-Vanishing-Culture-A-Language-Worth-Saving-1080.png)](https://futureknowledge.transistor.fm/episodes/vanishing-culture-5-a-language-worth-saving-with-peter-scholing)
Languages can vanish just as easily as books, websites, or archives—and when they do, entire ways of understanding the world can disappear with them. In the fifth episode of our special six-part series on _Vanishing Culture_, host Vida Vojić speaks with Peter Scholing of Biblioteca Nacional Aruba and the Coleccion Aruba digital heritage platform about the importance of preserving Papiamento, Aruba’s creole language, and the cultural heritage it carries.

Peter shares the story of how Aruba’s libraries, archives, and cultural institutions came together to build a digital collection that now preserves hundreds of thousands of documents, photographs, newspapers, recordings, and books. The conversation explores the unique preservation challenges facing island communities, from geographic isolation and climate risks to the legacy of colonialism, which has scattered much of Aruba’s historical record across multiple countries and languages. Peter explains why expanding access to materials in Papiamento is essential for education, cultural identity, and historical understanding, and how digitization is helping unlock previously inaccessible collections. Looking ahead, he discusses the opportunities new technologies offer for smaller languages and why ensuring a vibrant digital future for Papiamento is a crucial part of preserving Aruba’s culture for generations to come.

# [The Hidden Political Costs of Vanishing Culture](https://blog.archive.org/2026/07/16/the-hidden-political-costs-of-vanishing-culture/)

 Posted on [July 16, 2026](https://blog.archive.org/2026/07/16/the-hidden-political-costs-of-vanishing-culture/ "12:00 pm") by [Chris Freeland](https://blog.archive.org/author/chrisfreeland/ "View all posts by Chris Freeland")

In a new article, “[The Political Threats of Vanishing Culture and the Need to Protect Our Future Memory](https://journals.library.wustl.edu/pollib/article/id/9287/),” Michael Menna and Lila Bailey argue that the shift from buying physical media to licensing digital content has created a first-order political crisis. The piece builds on the groundwork laid in [_Vanishing Culture: A Report on Our Fragile Cultural Record_](https://archive.org/details/vanishing-culture-2026), tracing the problem across five arenas—educational resources, journalism, civic and movement archives, religious study, and environmental science—to show how digital ephemerality is already undermining people’s ability to learn, organize, and hold power accountable.

The article’s second half revisits the four digital rights at the heart of the [Our Future Memory](https://ourfuturememory.org/) movement: the rights to collect, preserve, provide access to, and collaborate around digital materials—the same things memory institutions have always done with physical collections. It walks through why each right matters on its own terms, from the Wiley e-book withdrawal that blindsided George Washington University to the federal government’s removal of climate data and environmental justice tools like EJScreen. The throughline is simple: none of this is inevitable. Vanishing culture is a policy choice, and it’s one we can still reverse if libraries, archives, and museums make the case together instead of absorbing these losses one at a time.

This piece is a call to action as much as an analysis. If you work at or care about a library, archive, or museum, we hope you’ll [read the full article](https://journals.library.wustl.edu/pollib/article/id/9287/) over at _The Political Librarian_, and consider signing the Statement on Digital Rights for Protecting Memory Institutions Online at [ourfuturememory.or](https://ourfuturememory.org/)g. The more institutions that add their names, the harder it becomes for publishers and platforms to treat our shared cultural memory as disposable.

# [Vanishing Culture Episode #3: Saving Queer Memory with Brooke Palmieri](https://blog.archive.org/2026/07/15/vanishing-culture-episode-3-saving-queer-memory-with-brooke-palmieri/)

 Posted on [July 15, 2026](https://blog.archive.org/2026/07/15/vanishing-culture-episode-3-saving-queer-memory-with-brooke-palmieri/ "12:05 pm") by [Chris Freeland](https://blog.archive.org/author/chrisfreeland/ "View all posts by Chris Freeland")

[![Image 13](https://blog.archive.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/FKP-Vanishing-Culture-Saving-Queer-Memory1080.png)](https://futureknowledge.transistor.fm/episodes/vanishing-culture-3-saving-queer-memory-with-brooke-palmieri)
Queer history has often been overlooked, erased, or excluded from traditional archives. In the third episode of our special six-part series on _Vanishing Culture_, host Vida Vojić speaks with writer, artist, and archivist Brooke Palmieri about the vital role queer archives play in preserving memory, identity, and community. Drawing on his work with CAMP BOOKS and his research into LGBTQ+ history, Brooke explores why archives matter—not just as collections of records, but as tools for helping people understand where they come from and imagine new futures.

**MORE INFO:**[https://futureknowledge.transistor.fm/episodes/vanishing-culture-3-saving-queer-memory-with-brooke-palmieri](https://futureknowledge.transistor.fm/episodes/vanishing-culture-3-saving-queer-memory-with-brooke-palmieri)

The conversation traces how queer communities have built their own archives in response to historical exclusion from mainstream institutions, creating spaces where stories of love, activism, creativity, and survival can be preserved and shared. Brooke reflects on the power of community-led archiving, the opportunities digital platforms create for expanding access to queer history, and the challenges posed by disappearing information, online misinformation, and unequal access to cultural heritage. Together, Vida and Brooke explore how preserving queer archives helps ensure that marginalized histories remain visible, accessible, and available to future generations.

_**Vanishing Culture**is a special six-part series from the[Future Knowledge podcast](https://futureknowledge.transistor.fm/), produced by the Internet Archive and Authors Alliance, exploring what happens when our shared cultural heritage disappears, and what we can do to preserve it.[View the full series](https://blog.archive.org/2026/06/30/introducing-the-vanishing-culture-podcast-series-exploring-what-we-stand-to-lose/)._

[![Image 14](https://blog.archive.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/VANISHING-CULTURE-COVER-new.png)](https://archive.org/details/vanishing-culture-2026)

The series is inspired by the Internet Archive’s new book,[**_Vanishing Culture_**](https://archive.org/details/vanishing-culture-2026), a collection of essays by scholars, artists, librarians, and preservationists examining why culture disappears in the digital age, and what can be done to save it. Together, the book and podcast offer complementary perspectives on one of the defining challenges of our time: preserving humanity’s cultural record in an increasingly fragile digital world.
