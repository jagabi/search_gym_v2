# Italy _, properly._

Italy is best planned as a small set of cities done properly, not a long list of stops crossed off. Rome, Florence, Venice, and a southern leg are the four classic anchors, and most strong trips pick two or three. The Frecciarossa makes city-to-city moves easy; the trip dies when you try to add a fifth.

Plan by intent
## Plan by intent

Three planning lenses for Italy. Use them to decide whether to add a destination — or stay put.

### First-time highlights

Rome, Florence, and Venice on the Frecciarossa corridor. Three to four nights in Rome, two in Florence, one to two in Venice. The single most reliable first-trip shape in Europe.

### Slower cultural depth

Pick two cities and stay longer in each. Rome plus Florence, or Florence plus a Tuscan slow leg, lets museums, neighbourhoods, and food cluster properly instead of being squeezed between transfers.

### Northern food + southern coast

Combine Bologna or Milan with a southern leg (Naples + the Amalfi coast). It's a less obvious shape and rewards travellers who want food-first northern Italy plus a coastal recovery week.

Planning FAQ
## Italy planning, asked & answered

PLANNING YOUR TRIP

How many days do you need for Italy?
Most first-time trips work well at 10 to 14 days. That gives Rome, Florence, and Venice space without rushing the rail moves. Shorter trips are better focused on one or two cities than splintered across three.

What is the best first-time Italy itinerary?
Rome, Florence, and Venice on the Frecciarossa is the classic — three nights in Rome, two in Florence, one to two in Venice. It's well-worn for a reason: it gives a clean cross-section of Italy without forcing logistics complexity.

Is it better to visit two cities or three?
Two cities with longer stays usually produce a stronger trip than three rushed ones. The exception is the Rome-Florence-Venice route, where the rail times are short enough that three works at 10+ days.

What is the biggest planning mistake?
Adding a fourth or fifth city. Italy looks small on a map but the cities are dense — each one needs at least two real days. Most over-planned Italy trips would have been better with one fewer stop.

GETTING AROUND

Should you rent a car in Italy?
Not for major cities. For Tuscany, the Amalfi coast, or Sicily, a car helps. For Rome-Florence-Venice, the train is faster, cheaper, and avoids ZTL (restricted traffic zone) fines.

Is the train easy in Italy?
Yes. The Frecciarossa and Italo high-speed trains are reliable, comfortable, and frequent. Book a few days ahead for the best fares; same-day prices can spike sharply.

Do you need to book the Vatican or Uffizi in advance?
Yes — both regularly sell out days ahead in peak season, and Uffizi same-day tickets often aren't available at all. Book timed-entry slots before booking the rest of the day.

TIMING & WEATHER

When is the best time to visit Italy?
April-May and September-October offer the best balance of weather, daylight, and crowd levels. June is still good but warming up; July and August are hot and tourist-heavy almost everywhere.

Is Venice flooded in winter?
Venice gets acqua alta (high water) most frequently from October to January, with November being the most flood-prone month. The MOSE barrier has helped, but the lowest areas (San Marco) still flood several times each winter.

WHERE TO GO

Is Rome or Florence better for a first trip?
Most first trips do both. Rome is bigger, more dramatic, more sprawling; Florence is smaller, more walkable, more concentrated. They complement each other rather than competing.

Should you visit the south?
If you have 10+ days, yes. Naples plus the Amalfi Coast, or Puglia, adds a slower coastal counterweight that contrasts well with the dense northern cities. On shorter trips, skip the south and stay focused.

Is Tuscany worth the detour?
Yes if you have a car and 3+ extra days. Tuscany works as a slow-leg countryside week, not as quick day trips from Florence — the value comes from staying overnight in a small hilltop town.

ON THE GROUND

Is Italy expensive?
Italy varies a lot by region. Venice, Florence centre, and the Amalfi coast are expensive; Rome is mid-range; Naples, Bologna, and Puglia are noticeably cheaper. Eating outside the main tourist zones makes the biggest cost difference.

Can you travel Italy without speaking Italian?
Yes. English is widely spoken in tourist areas and major cities. Smaller towns and family-run restaurants in the south can be more limited, but it's rarely a real obstacle for travellers.

Is Italy safe for tourists?
Italy is generally very safe. The main risk in Rome, Florence, and Naples is pickpocketing, especially on public transport and around major sights. Standard precautions handle it.
