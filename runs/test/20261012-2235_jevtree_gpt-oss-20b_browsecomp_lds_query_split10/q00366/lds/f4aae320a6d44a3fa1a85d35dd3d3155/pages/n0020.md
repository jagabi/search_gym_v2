From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Magnetic tape used for storing video and sound simultaneously

For the song by Radiohead, see [In Rainbows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_Rainbows "In Rainbows").

[![Image 4](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f7/Assorted_video_tapes.JPG/330px-Assorted_video_tapes.JPG?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Assorted_video_tapes.JPG)

An assortment of videotapes

**Videotape** is [magnetic tape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_tape "Magnetic tape") used for storing [video](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video "Video") and usually [sound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_recording_and_reproduction "Sound recording and reproduction") in addition. Information stored can be in the form of either an [analog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analog_signal "Analog signal") or [digital signal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_signal_(signal_processing) "Digital signal (signal processing)"). The tape can come in a stand-alone tape reel or inside a casing such as a [tape cartridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tape_cartridge "Tape cartridge") or [cassette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassette_(format) "Cassette (format)"). Videotape is used in both [video tape recorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_tape_recorder "Video tape recorder") (VTRs) and, more commonly, [videocassette recorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videocassette_recorder "Videocassette recorder") (VCRs) and [camcorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camcorders "Camcorders"). Videotapes have also been used for storing scientific or medical data, such as the data produced by an [electrocardiogram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrocardiogram "Electrocardiogram").

Because video signals have a very high [bandwidth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bandwidth_(signal_processing) "Bandwidth (signal processing)"), and [stationary heads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tape_head "Tape head") would require extremely high tape speeds, in most cases, a [helical-scan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helical_scan "Helical scan") video head rotates against the moving tape to record the data in two dimensions.

Tape is a [sequential access](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequential_access "Sequential access") method of storing information and thus imposes delays to access a portion of the tape that is not already against the heads. The early 2000s saw the introduction and rise to prominence of high-quality [random-access](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random-access "Random-access") video recording media such as [hard disks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_disk "Hard disk") and [flash memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flash_memory "Flash memory"). Since then, videotape has been increasingly relegated to [archival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archival "Archival") and similar uses.

## Early formats

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Videotape&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Early formats")]

The electronics division of entertainer [Bing Crosby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bing_Crosby "Bing Crosby")'s production company, Bing Crosby Enterprises (BCE), gave the world's first demonstration of a videotape recording in Los Angeles on November 11, 1951. In development by [John T. Mullin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_T._Mullin "John T. Mullin") and Wayne R. Johnson since 1950, the device gave what were described as "blurred and indistinct" images using a modified [Ampex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ampex "Ampex") 200 tape recorder and standard one-quarter-inch (0.64 cm)[audiotape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audiotape "Audiotape") moving at 360 inches (9.1 m) per second.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-1)[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-2) A year later, an improved version using one-inch (2.5 cm) magnetic tape was shown to the press, who reportedly expressed amazement at the quality of the images, although they had a "persistent grainy quality that looked like a worn motion picture." Overall, the picture quality was still considered inferior to the best [kinescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinescope "Kinescope") recordings on film.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-3) Bing Crosby Enterprises hoped to have a commercial version available in 1954, but none came forth.[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-4)

The [BBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC "BBC") experimented from 1952 to 1958 with a high-speed linear videotape system called [Vision Electronic Recording Apparatus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vision_Electronic_Recording_Apparatus "Vision Electronic Recording Apparatus") (VERA), but this was ultimately dropped in favor of [quadruplex videotape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadruplex_videotape "Quadruplex videotape"). VERA used one-half-inch (1.3 cm) tape on 20-inch (51 cm) reels traveling at 200 inches per second (5.1 m/s).

[RCA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RCA_Corporation "RCA Corporation") demonstrated the magnetic tape recording of both black-and-white and [color television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_television "Color television") programs at its [Princeton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey "Princeton, New Jersey") laboratories on December 1, 1953.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-5)[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-6) The high-speed longitudinal tape system, called Simplex, in development since 1951, could record and play back only a few minutes of a [television program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_program "Television program"). The color system used one-half-inch (1.3 cm) tape on 10+1⁄2-inch (270 mm) reels to record five tracks, one each for red, blue, green, synchronization, and audio. The black-and-white system used one-quarter-inch (0.64 cm) tape also on 10+1⁄2-inch (270 mm) reels with two tracks, one for video and one for audio. Both systems ran at 360 inches per second (9.1 m/s) with 2,500 feet (760 m) per reel yielding an 83-second capacity.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-7) RCA-owned [NBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NBC "NBC") first used it on _The Jonathan Winters Show_ on October 23, 1956, when a prerecorded song sequence by [Dorothy Collins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorothy_Collins "Dorothy Collins") in color was included in the otherwise [live television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Live_television "Live television") program.[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-8)[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-9)

In 1953, Norikazu Sawazaki developed a prototype [helical scan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helical_scan "Helical scan") videotape recorder.[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-10)

BCE demonstrated a color system in February 1955 using a [longitudinal recording](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitudinal_Video_Recording "Longitudinal Video Recording") on one-half-inch (one-point-three-centimetre) tape. [CBS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CBS "CBS"), RCA's competitor, was about to order BCE machines when [Ampex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ampex "Ampex") introduced the superior [Quadruplex system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadruplex_videotape "Quadruplex videotape").[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-11) BCE was acquired by [3M](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3M "3M") Company in 1956.

In 1959, [Toshiba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toshiba "Toshiba") released the first commercial helical scan videotape recorder.[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-12)

### Quad

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Videotape&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Quad")]

[![Image 5](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b9/2-inch_Quad_Tape_Reel_with_miniDV_cassette.jpg/250px-2-inch_Quad_Tape_Reel_with_miniDV_cassette.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:2-inch_Quad_Tape_Reel_with_miniDV_cassette.jpg)

A 14-inch (36 cm) reel of 2-inch (5.1 cm) videotape compared with a [MiniDV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MiniDV "MiniDV") videocassette of the 1990s. Both media store one hour of color video.

The first commercial professional [broadcast quality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadcast_quality "Broadcast quality") videotape machines capable of replacing [kinescopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinescope "Kinescope") were the two-inch (5.1 cm)[quadruplex videotape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadruplex_videotape "Quadruplex videotape") (Quad) machines introduced by [Ampex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ampex "Ampex") on April 14, 1956, at the [National Association of Broadcasters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Association_of_Broadcasters "National Association of Broadcasters") convention in [Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago "Chicago"). Quad employed a transverse (scanning the tape across its width) four-head system on a two-inch (5.1 cm) tape and stationary heads for the soundtrack.

[CBS Television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CBS_Television "CBS Television") first used the Ampex VRX-1000[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-nasginsburg-13) Mark IV at its Television City studios in Hollywood on November 30, 1956, to play a delayed broadcast of _[Douglas Edwards and the News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas\_Edwards\_and\_the\_News "Douglas Edwards and the News")_ from [New York City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City "New York City") to the [Pacific Time Zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_Time_Zone "Pacific Time Zone").[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-nasginsburg-13)[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-14) On January 22, 1957, the [NBC Television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NBC_Television "NBC Television") game show _[Truth or Consequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truth\_or\_Consequences "Truth or Consequences")_, produced in Hollywood, became the first program to be broadcast in all time zones from a prerecorded videotape.[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-15)

Ampex introduced a color videotape recorder in 1958 in a cross-licensing agreement with RCA, whose engineers had developed it from an Ampex black-and-white recorder.[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-16) NBC's special, _[An Evening With Fred Astaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An\_Evening\_With\_Fred\_Astaire "An Evening With Fred Astaire")_ (1958), is the second oldest surviving [television network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_network "Television network") color videotape,[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-17) and has been restored by the [UCLA Film and Television Archive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UCLA_Film_and_Television_Archive "UCLA Film and Television Archive").

On December 7, 1963, [instant replay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instant_replay "Instant replay"), originally a videotape-based system, was used for the first time during the live transmission of the [Army–Navy Game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Army%E2%80%93Navy_Game "Army–Navy Game") by its inventor, director [Tony Verna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Verna "Tony Verna").[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-18)

Although Quad became the industry standard for approximately thirty years, it has drawbacks such as an inability to freeze pictures and no picture search.[[a]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-20) Also, in early machines, a tape could reliably be played back using only the same set of hand-made tape heads, which wore out very quickly.[[b]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-21) Despite these problems, Quad is capable of producing excellent images. Subsequent videotape systems have used helical scan, where the video heads record diagonal tracks (of complete fields) onto the tape.

[Many early videotape recordings were not preserved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_lost_television_broadcasts "List of lost television broadcasts"). While much less expensive (if repeatedly recycled) and more convenient than kinescope, the high cost of [3M](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3M "3M") Scotch 179[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-nasginsburg-13) and other early videotapes ($300 per one-hour reel)[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-bfi-22) meant that most broadcasters [erased and reused](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_television_broadcast "Lost television broadcast") them, and (in the United States) regarded videotape as simply a better and more cost-effective means of time-delaying broadcasts than kinescopes. It was the four time zones of the continental United States that had made the system very desirable in the first place.

Some early broadcast videotapes have survived, including _[The Edsel Show](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The\_Edsel\_Show "The Edsel Show")_, broadcast live on October 13, 1957 and _[An Evening With Fred Astaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An\_Evening\_With\_Fred\_Astaire "An Evening With Fred Astaire")_ which aired on October 18, 1958 and was the oldest color videotape of an entertainment program known to exist until the discovery of the October 8, 1958 episode of the _[Kraft Music Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kraft\_Music\_Hall\_(TV\_series) "Kraft Music Hall (TV series)")_ hosted by [Milton Berle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milton_Berle "Milton Berle"). The oldest color videotape known to survive is the May 1958 dedication of the [WRC-TV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WRC-TV "WRC-TV") studios in [Washington, D.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington,_D.C. "Washington, D.C.") In 1976, [NBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NBC "NBC")'s 50th-anniversary special included an excerpt from a 1957 color special starring [Donald O'Connor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_O'Connor "Donald O'Connor"); despite some obvious technical problems, the color tape was remarkably good. Some classic television programs recorded on studio videotape have been made available on DVD – among them NBC's _[Peter Pan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter\_Pan\_(1954\_musical) "Peter Pan (1954 musical)")_ (first telecast in 1960) with [Mary Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Martin "Mary Martin") as Peter, several episodes of [The Dinah Shore Chevy Show](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dinah_Shore_Chevy_Show "The Dinah Shore Chevy Show") (late 1950s/early 60s), the final [Howdy Doody Show](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howdy_Doody_Show "Howdy Doody Show") (1960), the television version of [Hal Holbrook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hal_Holbrook "Hal Holbrook")'s one-man show _[Mark Twain Tonight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark\_Twain\_Tonight "Mark Twain Tonight")_ (first telecast in 1967), and [Mikhail Baryshnikov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Baryshnikov "Mikhail Baryshnikov")'s classic production of the ballet _[The Nutcracker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The\_Nutcracker "The Nutcracker")_ (first telecast in 1977).

### Types C and B

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Videotape&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Types C and B")]

The next format to gain widespread usage was 1-inch (2.5 cm)[Type C videotape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_C_videotape "Type C videotape") introduced in 1976. This format introduced features such as shuttling, various-speed playback (including slow-motion), and still framing. Although 1 in Type C's quality was still quite high, the sound and picture reproduction attainable on the format were of slightly lower quality than Quad. However, compared to Quad, 1 in Type C machines required much less maintenance, took up less space, and consumed much less electrical power.

In Europe, a similar tape format was developed, called 1 in[Type B videotape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_B_videotape "Type B videotape"). Type B machines use the same 1-inch tape as Type C, but they lack C's shuttle and slow-motion options. The picture quality is slightly better, though. Type B was the broadcast norm in continental Europe for most of the 1980s.

### Professional cassette formats

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Videotape&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Professional cassette formats")]

[![Image 6](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f8/U-matic.jpg/250px-U-matic.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:U-matic.jpg)

[U-matic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U-matic "U-matic") tape

A **videocassette** is a case containing videotape. In 1969, [Sony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sony "Sony") introduced a prototype for the first widespread video cassette, the 3⁄4 in (1.9 cm)[composite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composite_video "Composite video")[U-matic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U-matic "U-matic") system, which Sony introduced commercially in September 1971 after working out industry standards with other manufacturers. Sony later refined it to _Broadcast Video U-matic_ (BVU). Sony continued its hold on the professional market with its ever-expanding 1⁄2 in (1.3 cm)[component video](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Component_video "Component video")[Betacam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betacam "Betacam") family introduced in 1982. This tape form factor would go on to be used for leading professional digital video formats. [Panasonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panasonic "Panasonic") had some limited success with its [MII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panasonic_MII "Panasonic MII") system, but never could compare to Betacam in terms of market share.

The next step was the [digital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_data "Digital data") revolution. Sony's [D-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D-1_(Sony) "D-1 (Sony)") was introduced in 1986 and featured uncompressed digital component recording. Because D-1 was extremely expensive, the composite [D-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D-2_(video) "D-2 (video)") (Sony, 1988) and [D-3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D-3_(video) "D-3 (video)") (Panasonic, 1991) were introduced soon after. Ampex introduced the first compressed component recording with its [DCT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DCT_(videocassette_format) "DCT (videocassette format)") series in 1992. Panasonic's [D-5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D5_HD "D5 HD") format was introduced in 1994. Like D-1, it is uncompressed, but much more affordable.

The [DV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DV_(video_format) "DV (video format)") standard, which debuted in 1995, was widely used both in its native form as [MiniDV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MiniDV "MiniDV") and in more robust professional variants.

In digital camcorders, Sony adapted the Betacam system with its [Digital Betacam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_Betacam "Digital Betacam") format in 1993, and in 1996 following it up with the cheaper [Betacam SX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betacam_SX "Betacam SX") and the 2000 [MPEG IMX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MPEG_IMX "MPEG IMX") format,[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-23) The semiprofessional DV-based [DVCAM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DVCAM "DVCAM") system was introduced in 1996. Panasonic used its DV variant DVCPRO for all professional cameras, with the higher-end format [DVCPRO50](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DVCPRO50 "DVCPRO50") being a direct descendant. [JVC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JVC "JVC") developed the competing [D9/Digital-S](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital-S "Digital-S") format, which compresses video data in a way similar to DVCPRO but uses a cassette similar to [S-VHS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-VHS "S-VHS") media. Many helical scan cassette formats, such as VHS and Betacam, use a head drum with heads that use [azimuth recording](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azimuth_recording "Azimuth recording"), in which the heads in the head drum have a gap that is tilted at an angle, and opposing heads have their gaps tilted so as to oppose each other.[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-poptronics-24)[[23]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-25)

### High definition

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Videotape&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: High definition")]

The introduction of [HDTV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HDTV "HDTV")[video production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_production "Video production") necessitated a medium for storing [high-definition video](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-definition_video "High-definition video"). In 1997, Sony supplemented its Betacam family with the HD-capable [HDCAM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HDCAM "HDCAM") standard and its higher-end cousin [HDCAM SR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HDCAM_SR "HDCAM SR") in 2003. Panasonic's competing HD format for its camcorders was based on DVCPRO and called [DVCPRO HD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DVCPRO_HD "DVCPRO HD"). For VTR and archive use, Panasonic expanded the D-5 specification to store compressed HD streams and called it [D-5 HD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D-5_HD "D-5 HD").

[Video 8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_8 "Video 8") (left), [VHS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VHS "VHS") (right) and [MiniDV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MiniDV "MiniDV") (bottom)

### Videocassette recorders

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Videotape&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Videocassette recorders")]

The first consumer [videocassette recorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videocassette_recorder "Videocassette recorder") (VCRs) used Sony [U-matic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U-matic "U-matic") technology and were launched in 1971. [Philips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philips "Philips") entered the domestic market the following year with the [N1500](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N1500 "N1500").[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-26) Sony's [Betamax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betamax "Betamax") (1975) and JVC's [VHS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VHS "VHS") (1976) created a mass-market for VCRs and the two competing systems battled the [videotape format war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape_format_war "Videotape format war"), which VHS ultimately won. In Europe, Philips had developed the [Video 2000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_2000 "Video 2000") format, which did not find favor with the TV rental companies in the UK and lost out to VHS.

At first, VCRs and videocassettes were very expensive, but by the late 1980s, the price had come down enough to make them affordable to a mainstream audience. Videocassettes finally made it possible for consumers to buy or rent a complete film and watch it at home whenever they wished, rather than going to a movie theater or having to wait until it was telecast. It gave birth to [video rental stores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_rental_store "Video rental store"). [Blockbuster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blockbuster_(retailer) "Blockbuster (retailer)"), the largest chain, existed from 1985 to 2010. It also made it possible for a VCR owner to begin [time shifting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_shifting "Time shifting") their viewing of films and other [television programs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_program "Television program"). This caused an enormous change in viewing practices, as one no longer had to wait for a repeat of a program that had been missed. The shift to home viewing also changed the movie industry's revenue streams, because home renting created an additional window of time in which a film could make money. In some cases, films that did only modestly in their theater releases went on to have strong performances in the rental market (e.g., [cult films](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cult_films "Cult films")).

VHS became the leading consumer tape format for [home movies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_movies "Home movies") after the [videotape format war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape_format_war "Videotape format war"), though its follow-ups [S-VHS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-VHS "S-VHS"), [W-VHS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W-VHS "W-VHS") and [D-VHS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D-VHS "D-VHS") never caught up in popularity. In the early 2000s, in the prerecorded video market, VHS began to be displaced by [DVD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DVD "DVD"). The DVD format has several advantages over VHS tape. A DVD is much better able to take repeated viewings than VHS tape. Whereas a VHS tape can be erased through [degaussing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degaussing "Degaussing"), DVDs and other optical discs are not affected by magnetic fields. DVDs can still be damaged by scratches. DVDs are smaller and take less space to store. DVDs can support both standard 4:3 and widescreen 16:9 screen aspect ratios, and DVDs can provide twice the video resolution of VHS. DVD supports [random access](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_access "Random access") while a VHS tape is restricted to [sequential access](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequential_access "Sequential access") and must be rewound. DVDs can have interactive menus, multiple language tracks, audio commentaries, [closed captioning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_captioning "Closed captioning") and subtitling (with the option of turning the subtitles on or off, or selecting subtitles in several languages). Moreover, a DVD can be played on a computer.

Due to these advantages, by the mid-2000s, DVDs were the dominant form of prerecorded video movies. Through the late 1990s and early 2000s, consumers continued to use VCRs to record over-the-air TV shows, because consumers could not make home recordings onto DVDs. This last barrier to DVD domination was broken in the late 2000s with the advent of inexpensive [DVD recorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DVD_recorder "DVD recorder") and [digital video recorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_video_recorder "Digital video recorder") (DVRs). In July 2016, the last known manufacturer of VCRs, [Funai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funai "Funai"), announced that it was ceasing VCR production.[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-27)

### Consumer and prosumer camcorders

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Videotape&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Consumer and prosumer camcorders")]

[![Image 8](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/27/DV_tape_sizes_2.jpg/250px-DV_tape_sizes_2.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:DV_tape_sizes_2.jpg)

DV cassettes left to right: DVCAM-L, DVCPRO-M, DVC/MiniDV

Early consumer [camcorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camcorders "Camcorders") used full-size VHS or Betamax cassettes. Later models switched to more compact formats, designed explicitly for smaller camcorder use, like [VHS-C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VHS-C "VHS-C") and [Video8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video8 "Video8"). VHS-C is a downsized version of VHS, using the same recording method and the same tape, but in a smaller cassette. It is possible to play VHS-C tapes in a regular VHS tape recorder by using an adapter. After the introduction of [S-VHS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-VHS "S-VHS"), a corresponding compact version, S-VHS-C, was released as well. Video8 is an indirect descendant of Betamax, using narrower tape and a smaller cassette. Because of its narrower tape and other technical differences, it is not possible to develop an adapter from Video8 to Betamax. Video8 was later developed into [Hi8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hi8 "Hi8"), which provides better resolution similar to S-VHS.

The first consumer-level and lower-end professional ([prosumer](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/prosumer#Etymology_2 "wikt:prosumer")) digital video recording format, introduced in 1995, used a smaller Digital Video Cassette (DVC).[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-28) The format was later renamed [MiniDV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MiniDV#MiniDV "MiniDV") to reflect the [DV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DV_(video_format) "DV (video format)") encoding scheme, but the tapes are still marked _DVC_. Some later formats like [DVCPRO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DVCPRO "DVCPRO") from Panasonic reflect the original name. The DVC or MiniDV format provides [broadcast-quality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadcast-quality "Broadcast-quality") video and sophisticated nonlinear editing capability on consumer and some professional equipment and has been used on feature films, including Danny Boyle's [28 Days Later](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/28_Days_Later "28 Days Later") (2002, shot on a Canon XL1)[[27]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-29)[[28]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-:2-30) and David Lynch's [Inland Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inland_Empire_(film) "Inland Empire (film)") (2006, shot on a Sony DSR-PD150).[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-Perez-31)

In 1999 Sony backported the DV recording scheme to 8-mm systems, creating [Digital8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital8 "Digital8"). By using the same cassettes as Hi8, many Digital8 camcorders were able to play analog Video8 or Hi8 recordings, preserving compatibility with already recorded [analog video](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analog_video "Analog video") tapes.

Sony introduced another camcorder cassette format called [MicroMV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MicroMV "MicroMV") in 2001. Sony was the only electronics manufacturer to sell MicroMV cameras. In 2006, Sony stopped offering new MicroMV camcorder models.[[30]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-derStandard-32) In November 2015, Sony announced that shipment of MicroMV cassettes would be discontinued in March 2016.[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-tele-33)[[32]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-34)[[33]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotape#cite_note-35)

In the late 2000s, [MiniDV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MiniDV "MiniDV") and its high-definition cousin, [HDV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HDV "HDV"), were the two most popular consumer or prosumer tape-based formats. The formats use different encoding methods, but the same cassette type.

## Future of tape

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Videotape&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Future of tape")]

See also: [Tapeless production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tapeless_production "Tapeless production")

With advances in technology, videotape has moved past its original uses (original recording, editing, and broadcast playback) and is now primarily an archival medium. The death of tape for video recording was predicted as early as 1995 when the Avid nonlinear editing system was demonstrated, storing video clips on hard disks. Yet videotape was still used extensively, especially by consumers, up until about 2004, when DVD-based camcorders became affordable and domestic computers had large enough hard drives to store an acceptable amount of video.

Consumer camcorders have switched from being tape-based to [tapeless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tapeless_camcorder "Tapeless camcorder") machines that record video as computer files. Small hard disks and writable optical discs have been used, with solid-state memory such as [SD cards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SD_cards "SD cards") being the current market leader. There are two primary advantages: First, copying a tape recording onto a computer or other video machine occurs in real time (e.g., a ten-minute video would take ten minutes to copy); since tapeless camcorders record video as computer-ready data files, the files can be copied onto a computer significantly faster than real time. Second, tapeless camcorders, and those using solid-state memory in particular, are far simpler mechanically and so are more reliable.

Despite these conveniences, tape is still used extensively by filmmakers and television networks because of its longevity, low cost, and reliability. Master copies of visual content are often stored on tape for these reasons, particularly by users who cannot afford to move to tapeless machines. During the mid- to late 2000s, professional users such as broadcast television were still using tape heavily, but tapeless formats like [P2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P2_(storage_media) "P2 (storage media)"), [XDCAM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XDCAM "XDCAM") and [AVCHD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AVCHD "AVCHD") were gaining broader acceptance.

While live recording has migrated to solid-state and hard disks, the high cost of solid-state drives and the limited shelf life of hard-disk drives make them less desirable for archival use, for which tape is still used.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation\_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
