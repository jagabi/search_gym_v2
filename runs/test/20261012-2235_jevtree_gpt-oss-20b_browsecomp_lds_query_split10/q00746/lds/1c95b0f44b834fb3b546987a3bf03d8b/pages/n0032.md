*   What is the Missouri River?  

The Missouri River, nicknamed "Big Muddy," is the longest tributary of the [Mississippi River](https://www.britannica.com/place/Mississippi-River) and the second longest river in North America. It begins in southwest Montana at the confluence of the Jefferson, Madison, and Gallatin rivers. The Missouri River flows for 2,315 miles (3,726 km). The Missouri–Red Rock River system has a total length of some 2,540 miles (4,090 km), making it the third longest system in North America. The river's drainage basin covers about 529,400 square miles of the Great Plains.

The river was named Peki-tan-oui on some early French maps, and the mouth of the river was first encountered by Europeans in 1673 by French explorers. Today, the Missouri River serves as a source of water and hydroelectricity, and it is also one of the country’s most important recreational resources.

*   Where does the Missouri River start and end?  

The Missouri River starts in southwestern Montana, formed by the joining of the Jefferson, Madison, and Gallatin rivers in the Rocky Mountains [about 4,000 feet (1,200 meters) above sea level](https://www.britannica.com/place/Missouri-River/Introduction#ref1). The Missouri River flows through the Great Plains. It flows generally eastward across Montana, North Dakota, South Dakota, Nebraska, Iowa, Kansas, and Missouri. The Missouri River ends when it [joins the Mississippi River](https://www.britannica.com/place/Missouri-River/Introduction#ref1) approximately 10 miles (16 km) north of St. Louis.

*   What are some important cities along the Missouri River?  

The Missouri River flows through or near several important cities. These include:

    *   **Great Falls**, Montana, where the river flows northeastward.

    *   **Bismarck**, North Dakota, located just north of where the river trends southward.

    *   **Pierre**, South Dakota, found where the Missouri River again begins to trend southeastward.

    *   **Omaha**, Nebraska, situated on the west bank of the Missouri River.

    *   **Council Bluffs**, Iowa, located opposite Omaha on the east bank of the river.

    *   **Kansas City**, Missouri, where the river turns east.

    *   **Jefferson City**, Missouri, where the river makes its final eastward turn.

    *   **St. Louis**, Missouri, near the confluence of the Missouri and Mississippi Rivers.

Other chief cities along the Missouri River are Williston, North Dakota; Nebraska City, Nebraska; Atchison and Leavenworth, Kansas; and Columbia and St. Charles, Missouri.

*   How has the Missouri River been important to transportation, history, and the environment?  

The Missouri River has played a crucial role in transportation, history, and the environment of the Great Plains.

### Transportation

The Missouri River became an economic force in the 1800s when steamboats began navigating its waters. Steamboats brought unprecedented prosperity and allowed riverbank plantations to ship crops directly. The river's significance declined with the rise of railroads, which were more efficient for east-west transport.

### History

The Missouri River was home to Native American tribes for millennia, including the Blackfeet, Hidatsa, and Crow. French explorers first encountered the river in 1673, and the Lewis and Clark Expedition explored it from mouth to headwaters in 1804–05.

### Environment

In 1944, Congress authorized a program for flood control and water resource development in the Missouri River basin, leading to the construction of dams and reservoirs. While these projects reduced flooding and provided irrigation and hydroelectricity, they also faced criticism for threatening animal species and causing siltation. Some environmentalists advocate returning the river to its natural cycle of flooding and drought.

**Missouri River**, longest tributary of the [Mississippi River](https://www.britannica.com/place/Mississippi-River) and second longest [river](https://www.britannica.com/science/river) in [North America](https://www.britannica.com/place/North-America). It is formed by the [confluence](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/confluence) of the [Jefferson](https://www.britannica.com/place/Jefferson-River), [Madison](https://www.britannica.com/place/Madison-River), and [Gallatin](https://www.britannica.com/place/Gallatin-River) rivers in the [Rocky Mountains](https://www.britannica.com/place/Rocky-Mountains) area of southwestern [Montana](https://www.britannica.com/place/Montana-state) (Gallatin county), U.S., about 4,000 feet (1,200 metres) above [sea level](https://www.britannica.com/science/sea-level). The Missouri proper has a total course of 2,315 miles (3,726 km). Some sources, however, give the combined lengths of the Missouri proper and the Red Rock River (the upper course of the Jefferson River) of southwestern Montana as that of the Missouri River itself, instead of identifying it as a river system made up of both streams. The Missouri–Red [Rock River](https://www.britannica.com/place/Rock-River) system has a total length of some 2,540 miles (4,090 km), making it the third longest system in North America.

The Missouri first flows northward and northeastward (via [Great Falls](https://www.britannica.com/place/Great-Falls-Montana)) through western Montana before turning eastward across the northern portion of the state. Shortly after entering western [North Dakota](https://www.britannica.com/place/North-Dakota), it begins to [trend](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/trend) southeastward before continuing southward just south of [Bismarck](https://www.britannica.com/place/Bismarck-North-Dakota) into northern and central [South Dakota](https://www.britannica.com/place/South-Dakota) to [Pierre](https://www.britannica.com/place/Pierre-South-Dakota), where it again begins to trend southeastward. Continuing through central and southern South Dakota, the river then forms a section of the South Dakota–[Nebraska](https://www.britannica.com/place/Nebraska-state) boundary, the Nebraska–[Iowa](https://www.britannica.com/place/Iowa-state) boundary, the Nebraska–[Missouri](https://www.britannica.com/place/Missouri-state) boundary, and the northern section of the [Kansas](https://www.britannica.com/place/Kansas)–Missouri boundary. At [Kansas City](https://www.britannica.com/place/Kansas-City-Kansas), Kansas, the river again turns to the east and, after flowing through [Kansas City](https://www.britannica.com/place/Kansas-City-Missouri), Missouri, meanders eastward across west-central Missouri before heading southeastward again to [Jefferson City](https://www.britannica.com/place/Jefferson-City). There it makes its final eastward turn, flowing until it joins the Mississippi River about 10 miles (16 km) north of [St. Louis](https://www.britannica.com/place/Saint-Louis-Missouri).

The river’s [drainage basin](https://www.britannica.com/science/drainage-basin) occupies about 529,400 square miles (1,371,100 square km) of the [Great Plains](https://www.britannica.com/place/Great-Plains), of which 2,550 square miles (16,840 square km) are in southern [Canada](https://www.britannica.com/place/Canada). The range of elevations within its [basin](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/basin) is considerable: from some 14,000 feet (4,300 metres) above sea level in the [Colorado](https://www.britannica.com/place/Colorado-state) Rockies near the [Continental Divide](https://www.britannica.com/place/Continental-Divide) to 400 feet (120 metres) where it joins the Mississippi. The flow of the Missouri and of most of its tributaries is exceedingly varied—the minimum flow being 4,200 cubic feet (120 cubic metres) per second and the maximum 900,000 cubic feet (25,500 cubic metres) per second. With unprotected slopes and violent fluctuations in flow, erosion and silting are major problems.

The Missouri was named Peki-tan-oui on some early French maps and, later, Oumessourit; it has been nicknamed “Big Muddy” because of the amount of solid matter it carries in [suspension](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/suspension). For millennia, the area around the upper Missouri River was home to [Native American](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Native-American) peoples such as the [Blackfeet](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Blackfoot-people), [Hidatsa](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Hidatsa), and [Crow](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Crow-people). The mouth of the river was first encountered by Europeans in 1673—by the French explorers [Jacques Marquette](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Jacques-Marquette) and [Louis Jolliet](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Louis-Jolliet) while they were canoeing down the Mississippi River. In the early 1700s French fur traders began to navigate upstream. The first exploration of the river from its mouth to its headwaters was made in 1804–05 during the [Lewis and Clark Expedition](https://www.britannica.com/event/Lewis-and-Clark-Expedition). For many years commerce on the river was restricted to the fur trade, and the river was little used by the earliest American settlers moving west. The [American Fur Company](https://www.britannica.com/money/American-Fur-Company) began to use steamers on the river in 1830. Steamboat traffic on the river reached its height in 1858 but began to decline in the following year with the completion of the Hannibal and St. Joseph Railway to [St. Joseph](https://www.britannica.com/place/Saint-Joseph-Missouri), Missouri.

[Fort Peck Dam on the Missouri River near Glasgow, northeastern Montana, U.S.](https://cdn.britannica.com/69/101769-050-E9E6AA13/Fort-Peck-Dam-Missouri-River-Glasgow-Lake-1940.jpg)(more)

For the first 150 years after settlement along the river, little was done to develop the Missouri as a useful waterway or as a source of irrigation and power. In 1944 the U.S. Congress authorized a [comprehensive](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/comprehensive) program for flood control and [water resource](https://www.britannica.com/science/water-resource) development in the Missouri River basin. It [envisioned](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/envisioned) a system of more than 100 dams and reservoirs on the Missouri and certain of its tributaries. Local flood protection, involving levees and bank stabilization, and a deeper river channel were provided on the Missouri itself from [Sioux City](https://www.britannica.com/place/Sioux-City), Iowa, to the Mississippi, a distance of 760 miles (1,220 km). By the time an even more ambitious plan—the Missouri River basin program called the Pick-Sloan plan—was adopted in the 1950s, channel maintenance had enabled commercial barge lines to begin operating on the Missouri in 1953. The major [dams](https://www.britannica.com/technology/dam-engineering) built on the Missouri were [Fort Peck](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Fort-Peck-Dam) (near Glasgow, Montana), Garrison (North Dakota), and Gavin’s Point, Fort Randall, and Oahe (South Dakota). The Fort Peck Dam is one of the largest earthfill dams in the world. The entire system of dams and reservoirs has greatly reduced flooding on the Missouri and provides water to irrigate millions of acres of cropland along the main river and its tributaries. Hydroelectric installations along the river generate electricity for many [communities](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/communities) along the river’s upper course.

In addition to the locations already mentioned, the other chief cities along the Missouri are [Williston](https://www.britannica.com/place/Williston-North-Dakota), North Dakota; [Council Bluffs](https://www.britannica.com/place/Council-Bluffs), Iowa; [Omaha](https://www.britannica.com/place/Omaha-city-Nebraska) and [Nebraska City](https://www.britannica.com/place/Nebraska-City), Nebraska; [Atchison](https://www.britannica.com/place/Atchison-Kansas) and [Leavenworth](https://www.britannica.com/place/Leavenworth-Kansas), Kansas; and [Columbia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Columbia-Missouri) and [St. Charles](https://www.britannica.com/place/Saint-Charles-Missouri), Missouri. The river is not only of great value as a source of water and hydroelectricity, but it is also one of the country’s most important recreational resources. A segment of free-flowing river in north-central Montana is a federally designated national wild and scenic river, and the portion of the Missouri and its [adjacent](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/adjacent) lands east of there to Fort Peck [constitutes](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/constitutes) Charles M. Russell National Wildlife Refuge. In addition, much of the river along the South Dakota–Nebraska border is within Missouri National Recreational River, a facility under the jurisdiction of the U.S. National Park Service. There also are numerous state and local parks and recreational areas along the length of the river.
