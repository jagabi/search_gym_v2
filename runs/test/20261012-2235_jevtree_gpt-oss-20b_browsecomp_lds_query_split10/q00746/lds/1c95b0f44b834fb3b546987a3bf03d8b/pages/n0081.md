 Top Questions 

### When did Minnesota become a state?

Minnesota became the 32nd state of the [United States](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-States) on May 11, 1858. It had become a territory in 1849. While the territory reached west to the [Missouri River](https://www.britannica.com/place/Missouri-River), Minnesota’s boundaries were cut back to the Red River when it became a state.

### Why are people protesting in Minnesota in 2026?

Minnesotans have been protesting the presence of agents from [Immigration and Customs Enforcement](https://www.britannica.com/topic/United-States-Immigration-and-Customs-Enforcement) (ICE) and other federal agencies in the state as part of [Operation Metro Surge](https://www.britannica.com/event/2025-26-Minnesota-ICE-Deployment), a federal crackdown on immigration that began in the Minneapolis area in late 2025. Residents have also protested over the chaotic and unsafe tactics used by federal immigration agents and the shooting deaths of [two U.S. citizens](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Who-Was-Renee-Nicole-Macklin-Good) in January 2026. One notable protest was held on January 23, 2026—dubbed the Day of Truth and Freedom, thousands of people participated in a march and rally in Minneapolis, which was accompanied by a statewide general strike.

### What are Minnesota’s major geographical features?

Minnesota is known for its extensive woodlands, fertile [prairies](https://www.britannica.com/science/prairie), and numerous lakes, earning it the nickname “Land of 10,000 Lakes.” It also features a [continental climate](https://www.britannica.com/science/humid-continental-climate) with cold [winters](https://www.britannica.com/science/winter) and warm [summers](https://www.britannica.com/science/summer-season).

### Which ethnic groups have significantly influenced Minnesota’s population?

Although Minnesota’s population includes a large number of people of [Scandinavian](https://www.britannica.com/place/Scandinavia) origin, [Germans](https://www.britannica.com/place/Germany/People#ref58003) constitute the largest single ethnic group. Other significant groups include [Swedes](https://www.britannica.com/place/Sweden/Climate#ref30527), [Norwegians](https://www.britannica.com/place/Norway/Climate#ref37914), [Finns](https://www.britannica.com/place/Finland/People#ref26120), [Poles](https://www.britannica.com/place/Poland/Climate#ref28236), [Bohemians](https://www.britannica.com/place/Bohemia), [Irish](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ireland/Plant-and-animal-life#ref23016), [French](https://www.britannica.com/place/France/People#ref41115), and [French Canadians](https://www.britannica.com/place/Quebec-province/Climate#ref43130).The Native American population in Minnesota is primarily [Ojibwe](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Ojibwe) (self-name Anishinaabe; also called Chippewa), about half of whom live in the Twin Cities area; most of the remainder live on reservations in rural Minnesota. Since the mid-1970s, [Hispanic](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Hispanic-American), Asian, and African immigrants have settled in Minnesota. Hispanics now comprise about 7 percent of the total population. [Vietnamese](https://www.britannica.com/place/Vietnam/Climate#ref52698), [Laotians](https://www.britannica.com/place/Laos/People#ref52500), [Cambodians](https://www.britannica.com/place/Cambodia/Climate#ref52455), and [Hmong](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Hmong) refugees immigrated to the Twin Cities area beginning in the late 1970s, and refugees from [Somalia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Somalia) began arriving in the 1990s. Minnesota now has one of the largest Hmong populations and the largest Somali population in the country.

### What is the significance of the Twin Cities region in Minnesota?

The Twin Cities region, comprising [Minneapolis](https://www.britannica.com/place/Minneapolis) and [St. Paul](https://www.britannica.com/place/Saint-Paul-Minnesota), is the major administrative, economic, and cultural hub of Minnesota. St. Paul is the state capital.

**Minnesota**, [constituent](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/constituent)[state](https://www.britannica.com/topic/state-United-States-political-subdivision) of the [United States](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-States) of America. It became the 32nd state of the union on May 11, 1858. A small extension of the northern boundary makes Minnesota the most northerly of the 48 conterminous U.S. states. (This peculiar protrusion is the result of a boundary agreement with [Great Britain](https://www.britannica.com/place/Great-Britain-island-Europe) before the area had been carefully surveyed.) Minnesota is one of the north-central states. It is bounded by the [Canadian](https://www.britannica.com/place/Canada) provinces of [Manitoba](https://www.britannica.com/place/Manitoba) and [Ontario](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ontario-province) to the north, by [Lake Superior](https://www.britannica.com/place/Lake-Superior-lake-North-America) and the state of [Wisconsin](https://www.britannica.com/place/Wisconsin) to the east, and by the states of [Iowa](https://www.britannica.com/place/Iowa-state) to the south and [South Dakota](https://www.britannica.com/place/South-Dakota) and [North Dakota](https://www.britannica.com/place/North-Dakota) to the west.

Minnesota’s thousands of rivers flow northward via the [Red](https://www.britannica.com/place/Red-River) and Rainy rivers to [Hudson Bay](https://www.britannica.com/place/Hudson-Bay), eastward through the [Great Lakes](https://www.britannica.com/place/Great-Lakes) to the [Atlantic Ocean](https://www.britannica.com/place/Atlantic-Ocean), and southward through the [Mississippi River](https://www.britannica.com/place/Mississippi-River) to the [Gulf of Mexico](https://www.britannica.com/place/Gulf-of-Mexico-Gulf-of-America). Indeed, Minnesota received its name from the [Dakota](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Oceti-Sakowin) word for the [Mississippi’s](https://www.britannica.com/place/Mississippi-state) major tributary in the state, the [Minnesota River](https://www.britannica.com/place/Minnesota-River), which means “sky-tinted water.”

Minnesota consists of extensive woodlands, fertile prairies, and innumerable lakes—the basis for one of the state’s nicknames, “Land of 10,000 Lakes,” though Minnesota actually has more than 10,000 lakes that are larger than 10 acres (4 hectares) in area. The nearly 5,000 square miles (13,000 square km) of inland fresh water is a dominant feature in Minnesota. Its climate is continental, with cold winters and warm summers. About one in four Minnesotans is at least partly of Scandinavian origin, but those of German descent [constitute](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/constitute) the single largest [ethnic group](https://www.britannica.com/topic/ethnic-group) in the state. By the end of the 20th century, services had become the dominant activity of Minnesota’s economy, surpassing farming, mining, and manufacturing, which had been the major sources of income for the state since settlement. [St. Paul](https://www.britannica.com/place/Saint-Paul-Minnesota) is the state capital, and the Twin Cities region ([Minneapolis](https://www.britannica.com/place/Minneapolis)–St. Paul) is the major administrative, economic, and cultural hub of Minnesota. Area 86,935 square miles (225,161 square km). Population (2020) 5,706,494; (2024 est.) 5,793,151.

Minnesota’s terrain stretches from the edge of a subarctic [forest](https://www.britannica.com/science/forest) to the heart of the [Corn Belt](https://www.britannica.com/place/Corn-Belt). Most of the state was covered by glaciers several times, and the land’s surface was shaped by the [alternate](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/alternate) freezing, thawing, and movement of those glaciers. Prominent geomorphic reminders of this glacial activity are the rolling farmlands, thousands of lakes, steep hillsides, and flat glacial lake and outwash plains that make up Minnesota’s present-day landscape. The state’s rich [prairie](https://www.britannica.com/science/prairie) soils developed on the finely ground mineral materials left by the retreating glaciers. Minnesota’s elevations range from 602 feet (184 meters) above [sea level](https://www.britannica.com/science/sea-level) at Lake Superior to 2,301 feet (701 meters) above sea level at Eagle Mountain, located about 12 miles (19 km) from the lake’s north shore.

The majority of Minnesota’s lakes are located in the areas of glacial moraine, where glaciers deposited hills of sand and gravel. Lakes more than 100 square miles (260 square km) in area include Red Lake, Mille Lacs Lake, Leech Lake, Lake Winnibigoshish, [Lake of the Woods](https://www.britannica.com/place/Lake-of-the-Woods), and [Rainy Lake](https://www.britannica.com/place/Rainy-Lake). The shoreline of [Lake Superior](https://www.britannica.com/place/Lake-Superior-lake-North-America), one of the largest freshwater lakes in the world, forms the state’s northeastern border for some 160 miles (260 km). In northeastern Minnesota there are stream valleys and deep, clear lakes that were [scoured](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/scoured) by glaciers from the granite bedrock.

[![Image 35: USA, United States watercolor map Illustration in rainbow colors](https://cdn.britannica.com/41/257741-131-8B99D74E/USA-United-States-watercolor-map-illustration.jpg) Britannica Quiz 50 States, 50 Fun Facts Quiz](https://www.britannica.com/quiz/50-states-50-fun-facts-quiz)
The largest glacial lake plain (more than 100,000 square miles [260,000 square km]) was formed by [Lake Agassiz](https://www.britannica.com/place/Lake-Agassiz), which held the meltwaters as the latest glaciers retreated northward some 8,000 years ago. The southern part of the former lake bed lies along the Minnesota–North Dakota border and is known as the Red River valley. Red Lake, Lake of the Woods, and Canada’s [Lake Winnipeg](https://www.britannica.com/place/Lake-Winnipeg) are all remnants of this huge body of glacial meltwater. Its southward drainage created the wide valley of the Minnesota River, the flow of which eventually reversed as ice blockage to the north melted.

Extreme southeastern Minnesota was the only part of the state to escape glaciation during the last [ice age](https://www.britannica.com/science/ice-age-geology). There, streams cut their way through layers of limestone, leaving [extensive](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/extensive) caverns beneath the surface and steep, rocky bluffs rising high above the valleys.

## Soils

The most fertile soils in Minnesota formed beneath the original grasslands of the south and west and are rich in organic matter and soluble minerals. Soils that formed under the original [coniferous forest](https://www.britannica.com/science/coniferous-forest) in northeastern Minnesota are light-colored, acidic, and low in organic matter. Those that formed under the original hardwood forest, which occupied a belt between the prairie and the coniferous forest, are intermediate in color and natural fertility.

## Climate

Temperature variations in Minnesota occur not only seasonally but also from one part of the state to another. Southern Minnesota is hot in the summer. In the northern regions of the state, frost is possible in any month.

Average daily maximum temperatures in July range from the mid-80s F (about 29 °C) in southern Minnesota to the low 70s F (about 21 °C) along the shore of Lake Superior. Average daily January highs range from the mid-20s F (about −4 °C) in the south to about 15 °F (−9 °C) in the north; minimums are from about 5 °F (−15 °C) to about −5 °F (−21 °C). Record-breaking lows have been known to occur; in 1996 a temperature of −60 °F (−51 °C) was recorded near the northeastern city of Tower. The average frost-free periods vary from fewer than 90 days in parts of the north to more than 160 days in parts of the south.

Average annual precipitation ranges from less than 20 inches (500 mm) in the northwest to more than 30 inches (750 mm) in the southeast. Average seasonal snowfall varies from less than 40 inches (1,000 mm) in the western part of the state to more than 70 inches (1,800 mm) in the northeastern tip. Many parts of Minnesota have [continuous](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/continuous) snow cover for at least 90 days, from about mid-December to mid-March.
