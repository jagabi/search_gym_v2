ADMINISTRATIVE HISTORY
The Minneapolis City Planning Commission advises the City Council on issues related to
development, zoning, and capital improvements. It is a citizen’s committee established in 1920
by the Charter of the City of Minneapolis, following the City Planning Act for Minnesota of
1919. The Commission is composed of ten members: the Mayor; four legal voters appointed by
the Mayor and approved by the City Council; a representative and appointee  from the Hennepin
County Commissioner  ; and representatives from the City Council, School Board, and  Park
Board. The Planning Commission  is staffed by the City Planning Department,  an agency of
engineers, architects, and surveyors  within the Mayor’s Office  . In 2003, the City Planning
Department became part of a larger office called the Department of Community Planning and
Economic Development.
During the mid-twentieth century, the City Planning Committee faced issues related to
population growth and increasing automobile use in Minneapolis. These issues included
overcrowding, traffic congestion, and ageing buildings. Through the Planning Department, they
conducted research and issued proposals for the construction of the Minneapolis-St. Paul airport,
new parking structures, and a trunk highway system. They also made recommendations for
redeveloping blighted neighborhoods, including making changes to city zoning and replacing
deteriorating buildings with new housing projects. In 1954, The Planning Commission published
an Official City Plan, which they revised in conversation with the public over the following
decade.
Even as the Planning Commission was engaged in this work, Minneapolis, like many American
cities, had begun to decline. Between 1953 and 1978, the city lost over one quarter of its

reduction to attempt to keep the city’s population at 370,000.

SCOPE AND CONTENT
The Minneapolis City Planning Commission collection  houses reports, plans, and proposals
created or approved  by the City Planning Commission  between 1934 and 1979. Taken together,
these materials document the  growth, decline, and  nascent resurgence of Minneapolis in that era.
It is an important resource for understanding the roots of contemporary neighborhood  and
transportation patterns  in Minneapolis. It is also useful in demonstrating  changes in urban
planning in one American city across  45 years and through alternating political parties.
The collection is arranged in  to three series with no subseries  . The series include:
Series 1: Works Progress Administration projects
Series 2: Postwar projects
Series 3: Plan for the 1980s
The “Works Progress Administration projects” series, which dates from 1934  -1937, includes
reports and surveys related to  Minnesota WPA projects 3417,  1968, and 5435. These projects
deal with population growth; the railway and state highway system  s; property, housing, and
taxes; and the construction of a Public Safety Building  . The reports were conducted by Planning
Engineer Herman E. Olson  during the term of Mayor George E. Leach  (R).

The “Postwar projects” series is the largest series in the collection. It dates from 1945 to 1964.
This series includes annual reports as well as reports and surveys prepared for the Postwar
Progress Committee, the Airport Commission, the Chamber of Commerce, and the Minneapolis
Housing and Redevelopment Agency. It also contains an incomplete version of the Official City
Plan of 1954, along with related publications about neighborhoods, housing, land use, and
zoning. These were prepared by Planning Engineer Herman E. Olson, Planning Director
Lawrence M. Irvin, and Planning Commission presidents Patrick H. Carr, Robert T. Jones,
Arnett W. Leslie, and Elwood B. Swanson, during the term of mayors Hubert Humphrey (DFL),
Eric G. Hoyer (DFL), and P. Kenneth Peterson (R). The series offers information about specific
neighborhoods, including the Franklin School, Sumner Field, and Glenwood areas of North
Minneapolis; Seward; Loring Park; the Central Business District; and the Lower Loop. It also
offers plans for major transportation networks, including the Minneapolis-St. Paul Airport, the
interstate highway system, and Minnesota State Highway 62.
The “Plan for the 1980s” series contains the background document “Planning for the 1980s:
Issues for Community Discussion” as well as an incomplete Hearing Draft of Plan for the 1980s.
