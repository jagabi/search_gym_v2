Born December 18, 1973, in Reading, Berkshire, Lucy Worsley grew up in a family where her geologist father, Peter Worsley, had a clear plan for her future, she was to become a scientist. Her childhood was peripatetic, with moves to Canada, Iceland, and Norway for her father's research on glaciers and permafrost, and she often felt like "the freak" at school, finding her real friends through the Girl Guides and music. She dutifully began A-levels in biology, chemistry, and maths, but after one term, her mother, who worked in educational policy, noticed her misery and suggested a switch to history. When she told her father, he delivered the line that would follow him for the rest of his life, "If you do a history degree, my girl, you'll be cleaning toilets for a living." Worsley ignored him, read Ancient and Modern History at New College, Oxford, graduated with First-class honours in 1995, and earned a PhD in art history from the University of Sussex. Her first job out of college was as a tour guide at Milton Manor, a "slightly bonkers" country house where she discovered her talent for acting out the past, and she later worked as an inspector of ancient monuments for English Heritage before joining Historic Royal Palaces in 2003, overseeing Hampton Court Palace and the Tower of London. In 2018 she was awarded an OBE for services to history and heritage, proving that history degrees do not lead to a mop and bucket. In November 2011, she married architect Mark Hines, who, before the wedding, made her sign a pre-nuptial agreement with one very specific clause, she was banned from ever appearing on Strictly Come Dancing, partly because contestants are notorious for running off with their professional partners, and partly because he feared the sequins and showmanship would go straight to her head. Worsley has also spoken candidly about her decision not to have children, a choice that made her an unwitting poster girl for opting out of motherhood and brought a flood of unsolicited advice from strangers, with complete strangers writing to tell her that her life would be a disaster. She has noted that while growing up in the 1980s, the overriding message from teachers and parents was to finish your education, and that early conditioning may have stuck in ways they had not intended. Today she is one of the most recognizable historians on television, a bestselling author, and a presenter who has brought the nitty-gritty details of everyday life in the past to millions of viewers, often dressed in period-accurate costumes that she calls "a fabulous little window into a lost world."
