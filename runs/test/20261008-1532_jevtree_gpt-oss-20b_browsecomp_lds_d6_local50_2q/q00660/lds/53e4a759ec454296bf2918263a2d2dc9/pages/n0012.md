[THE SURFACE IS NEVER THE FULL STORY My process is deliberately repetitive. I work through accumulation. A mark sits on another mark. A layer covers another layer. Something disappears and then returns. Sometimes I don’t know exactly what will emerge. That is part of the process. The surface becomes a record of decisions, revisions, concealments and discoveries. In this sense, the painting behaves like memory. We remember selectively. We forget selectively. Some things remain clear. Others become fragments. Some memories are covered by newer experiences but never completely disappear. The same thing happens culturally. A building may be destroyed, but photographs remain. A ritual may disappear, but its symbols remain. A language may weaken, but certain words survive. A story may no longer be told in its original form, but traces of it remain inside the way people behave. The archive is not always a book. Sometimes the archive is a wall. Sometimes it is a pattern. Sometimes it is a proverb. Sometimes it is a ritual. Sometimes it is a painting. #powerofart #culturalfoundations #history #gbolahanayoola](https://www.facebook.com/100064379425894/videos/1397872649134793/?__so__=watchlist&__rv__=video_home_www_playlist_video_list&__cft__[0]=AZhtgMhJ7_k4JHVcKtN9-NfAQjUgeTKn2S1W05n_E_I452pnnn-ddJlBG07zvHe0kexT6gtLoKbOI27ePcpqxwO9sxXMHOIkrwcwGSQD7E6hMA9ZgC7pNJenAS3Cw6Rvciw0WFM9VTC_EvNAFXRtzWmG4rggO6n6p-w8uJml1-rZrswSIKsHy0NuRkxoqNCZ5Cvruexnxg)

For me, it is more than a medium it carries memory, place, ancestry and the physical realities from which culture is formed. In this body of work, earth becomes a language through which I reflect on the foundations that hold communities together: inherited knowledge, identity, environment and lived history. To have Prof. Onabrakpeya whose practice has continually expanded the possibilities of material, printmaking and African visual language pause to interrogate the work was both humbling and affirming. [My interest lies in advancing the importance of archaeology and preservation as it relates to human history in Nigeria and across Africa. For too long, conversations about African history have leaned heavily on oral and literary traditions. While these forms are rich and meaningful, they are also vulnerable to distortion over time. Memory bends, emphasis shifts, and each generation reshapes what it inherits. This does not invalidate these forms of documentation, but it reveals their limits when used in isolation. What strengthens history is corroboration. What stabilises identity is evidence.
