# Native American Urban Relocation

## [](https://www.archives.gov/education/lessons/indian-relocation.html)Native American Urban Relocation

The U.S. government's efforts to assimilate Native Americans into mainstream culture can be seen throughout the 20th century in the Bureau of Indian Affairs (BIA) activities. In 1953, the U.S. Congress established a new policy towards Native Americans: termination. This policy eliminated much government support for Native tribes and ended the protected trust status of all Native American-owned lands.

In response to this policy, the BIA began a voluntary urban relocation program. Native Americans could move from their rural tribes to metropolitan areas such as Chicago, Denver, Los Angeles, Cleveland, and Seattle. BIA pledged assistance with locating housing and employment. Numerous Native Americans made the move to cities. They struggled to adjust to life in a metropolis and faced unemployment, low-end jobs, discrimination, homesickness and the loss of traditional cultural supports. The urban relocation program changed the face of cities as well as Native American culture. Native Americans, who returned to the reservation often, found they did not "fit in" with those who stayed behind. When BIA urban relocation efforts started nearly eight percent of Native Americans lived in cities. The 2000 Census noted that Native American population had risen to approximately sixty-four percent.

The Chicago Field Office Employment Assistance Case Files contain memorandums and progress reports, as well as promotional devices used to recruit Native Americans on reservations. To comply with privacy exemptions found in the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) 5 U.S.C. 552, the names of individuals has been covered up for classroom use. The Chicago BIA Relocation Field Office created and distributed the suburban posters after the Chicagoland poster.

### [](https://www.archives.gov/education/lessons/indian-relocation.html)Guided Questions

*   What is the most prevalent tribe listed on the poster?
*   What types of jobs did "Chicagoland Indians" get?
*   Would you consider these "good jobs" as the poster states? Why/Why not?
*   Where do you think a poster like this was used?
*   Even though they are covered on your copy, why would this poster have listed individual names?
*   Compare the "Chicagoland Indians" poster to "Family in Waukegan and Elgin" poster. What similarities and differences do you notice in the messages and persuasive techniques?
*   What insights do these documents provide about the relationship between the federal government and Native Americans during this time period?

### [](https://www.archives.gov/education/lessons/indian-relocation.html)Extension Activities

_Further Research:_

Encourage students to explore through National Archives records life on reservations and in major cities during the 1950s. Focus specifically on the tribes listed on the posters. What would motivate Native Americans to leave their communities and move to a major city? What social issues were prevalent in cities, such as Chicago during the 1950s?

_Creative Writing:_

Write a letter in the voice of one of the members listed on either poster to relatives living on the reservation about experiences in Chicago.

_Further Research:_

Using other National Archives resources, chart the relationship the Federal government had with Native Americans.
